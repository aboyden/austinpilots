Section 1

The Pitot-Static System

Three of your six primary flight instruments run entirely on air pressure — no electricity, no vacuum pump. They're driven by two types of pressure that your airplane collects from the outside air: ram air pressure from the pitot tube (air being forced in as you fly forward) and static pressure from the static ports (ambient air pressure around the fuselage).

The pitot tube points forward into the slipstream and collects dynamic pressure — a combination of ram air and static. The static ports, usually flush with the fuselage side, collect only ambient pressure. The difference between the two is impact pressure, which is directly proportional to your airspeed.

📡 Pitot Tube

Faces forward. Collects total pressure (ram + static). Feeds only the airspeed indicator. Has a drain hole at the bottom — water exits here. Usually heated electrically to prevent ice blockage.

🔵 Static Port

Flush with fuselage, usually one or two ports on each side. Feeds all three pitot-static instruments: airspeed indicator, altimeter, and VSI. Blocked static = all three affected.

🌡️ Airspeed Indicator

Measures the difference between pitot (total) pressure and static pressure. Needs both pitot and static. The only instrument that uses pitot pressure directly.

🏔️ Altimeter & VSI

Use static pressure only. The altimeter senses absolute pressure; the VSI senses the rate of change. Neither cares about pitot tube pressure.

Most aircraft have an alternate static source — usually inside the cockpit — that you can switch to if the primary static port becomes blocked. Cabin pressure is slightly lower than outside air due to venturi effects around openings, so using alternate static will cause the altimeter to read slightly higher than actual and airspeed to read slightly higher as well. The VSI will momentarily spike then re-zero. Know your aircraft's POH for specific errors.

Alternate Static — No Alternate Source Aircraft
If you have no alternate static source and your static port blocks, you can break the face of the VSI (it's connected to static). This converts the VSI into a makeshift static port, restoring the altimeter and airspeed indicator — at the cost of the VSI. Know this procedure before you need it.
Section 2

The Six Pack — Gyroscopic Instruments

The other three instruments in the standard "six pack" — the attitude indicator, heading indicator, and turn coordinator — use gyroscopes. Gyros work on two principles: rigidity in space (a spinning gyro resists any force trying to change its orientation) and precession (a force applied to a spinning gyro results in movement 90° in the direction of rotation).

Power Sources — Why It Matters

Gyroscopic instruments need a power source to spin their rotors. In most light aircraft, two different power sources are used — which is intentional. If one fails, you don't lose all your gyros at once.

InstrumentPower SourceNotes
Attitude IndicatorVacuum (suction)Most common. Electric AI available in some aircraft.
Heading IndicatorVacuum (suction)Precesses over time — must be aligned to magnetic compass every 15 min.
Turn CoordinatorElectric (battery/alternator)Intentionally on a different power source than AI/HI for redundancy.
Airspeed / Altimeter / VSINo power neededPitot-static: pure pneumatics, no electrical or vacuum requirement.
The Redundancy Design
The turn coordinator runs on electricity; the AI and HI run on vacuum. If you lose your vacuum pump, you still have the turn coordinator for bank information. If you lose electrical power, you still have the AI and HI. The pitot-static instruments need neither — they work as long as the tubes are clear and unblocked.
Section 3

Instrument Errors You Need to Know

Magnetic Compass Errors

The magnetic compass is your ultimate backup — it needs no electricity, no vacuum, no gyro. But it has several well-known errors that make it unreliable during turns and accelerations:

🔄 Turning Errors

OSUN / ANDS: When turning through North, the compass lags (under-reads the turn). When turning through South, it leads (over-reads). This error is greatest at the poles and zero at the equator. In the US, the rule is: turning to North, roll out early; turning to South, roll out late.

⚡ Acceleration Errors

ANDS: Accelerate = North; Decelerate = South. On easterly or westerly headings, accelerating makes the compass swing toward North; decelerating swings it toward South. No error on North/South headings.

🔩 Magnetic Deviation

Metal and electrical fields in the aircraft cause the compass needle to deviate from magnetic north. A deviation card on the instrument shows the correction for each heading. Required by regulation to be checked and posted in the aircraft.

🌍 Magnetic Variation

Not an instrument error — it's the angular difference between true and magnetic north. Varies by location; isogonic lines on sectionals show equal-variation areas. "East is least, West is best" (for converting true to magnetic).

Memory Aid: OSUN / ANDS
Overshoot South, Undershoot North — when turning north, stop short of north; when turning south, go past south.
Accelerate North, Decelerate South — on east/west headings, acceleration swings the card toward North, deceleration toward South.

Heading Indicator Precession

The heading indicator (HI) uses a gyro that tends to drift over time due to bearing friction and gyroscopic precession. In IMC, you must re-align the HI to the magnetic compass every 15 minutes on straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight. If you forget, you may be navigating on a heading that's off by several degrees — which matters enormously on approaches.

Attitude Indicator Errors

The AI can show errors during prolonged coordinated turns, skids, or during initial gyro spinup. After engine start, wait for the AI to fully erect (usually 5 minutes) before using it for IFR flight. During steep prolonged turns, it may precess slightly — cross-check with the turn coordinator.

Section 4

Recognizing Instrument Failures

The dangerous thing about instrument failure in IMC isn't the failure itself — it's not noticing it. A stuck altimeter, a frozen VSI, or a slowly tumbling AI can mislead you for minutes before you realize something is wrong. The cure is a disciplined instrument scan and knowing what each failure looks like.

FailureSymptomsWhat to Do
Pitot blocked (open drain) ASI reads zero or drops to zero Turn on pitot heat. Use power settings from memory. Altimeter and VSI work normally.
Pitot blocked (drain also blocked) ASI acts like an altimeter — increases in climb, decreases in descent This is insidious — pitot heat essential. If climbing, ASI "reads" fast; descending, reads slow.
Static blocked ASI reads incorrectly; altimeter freezes; VSI reads zero Switch to alternate static source. If none, break the VSI glass as a last resort.
Vacuum failure AI and HI slowly become unreliable; turn coordinator still works Switch to partial panel. Cover AI/HI if they give conflicting info. Use TC for bank reference.
AI tumble (unusual attitude) AI shows extreme bank/pitch that may not match other instruments Trust the TC and ASI/altimeter trend. Unusual attitude recovery on partial panel.
Electrical failure Turn coordinator fails; radios out; AI and HI still work (if vacuum OK) Bank reference now from AI only. Declare emergency if needed. Land ASAP.
The Frozen Static Port Trap
Ice in the static port is silent and invisible. The altimeter freezes at the altitude where it blocked. The ASI starts reading incorrectly — too fast when descending, too slow when climbing. The VSI goes to zero. If you see a frozen altimeter and zero VSI, suspect a blocked static port first, then switch to alternate static.
Section 5

Partial Panel Flying

If your vacuum system fails in IMC, you lose the attitude indicator and heading indicator. You're now flying "partial panel" — navigating and maintaining control using only the pitot-static instruments and the electrically-powered turn coordinator. It's one of the hardest skills in instrument flying, and one of the most important.

Your Remaining Instruments

↩️ Turn Coordinator

Your primary bank reference. The miniature airplane shows rate and direction of turn. The ball tells you if the turn is coordinated. This becomes your primary attitude reference for bank.

💨 Airspeed Indicator

Primary pitch reference — indirectly. Increasing speed means nose is low; decreasing means nose high. Combined with power setting, it tells you your pitch attitude.

🏔️ Altimeter

Confirms pitch trend. Altitude increasing = climbing = nose up. Altitude decreasing = descending = nose low. Use it as a pitch cross-check along with ASI and VSI.

🧭 Magnetic Compass

Your only heading reference (with its errors). Plan turns carefully, accounting for OSUN. Turn to a heading, roll out at the right point, then verify on compass when stable.

Turning to a Heading on Partial Panel

Without the heading indicator, you use the magnetic compass — but you have to account for its turning errors. Here's the technique for turning to a specific heading in the northern hemisphere:

1
Determine the shortest turn direction to your desired heading.
2
Begin a standard rate turn (3°/sec) using the turn coordinator. Time the turn: 10 seconds = 30°.
3
If turning through North: roll out approximately 30° before the desired heading (in the northern US). The compass will continue to swing and settle on your heading.
4
If turning through South: roll out approximately 30° after the desired heading. The compass will settle back to your heading.
5
Wait for the compass to stabilize in straight-and-level flight before reading it. Any bank or acceleration will give you a false reading.
The Timed Turn Method
On partial panel, use the clock: a standard rate turn (3°/sec) takes exactly 20 seconds to turn 60°, 30 seconds for 90°, and 60 seconds for 180°. Time your turns instead of reading the compass in a bank. Roll out, then verify on the (now-stable) compass.

Unusual Attitude Recovery on Partial Panel

1
Nose-low, high speed, increasing altitude loss: Reduce power immediately. Level the wings using the turn coordinator. Gently raise the nose. Don't pull hard — in a bank, you're just tightening the spiral.
2
Nose-high, low speed, altitude increasing or decelerating: Add full power. Level the wings. Lower the nose until airspeed builds to normal cruise.
3
Always use the turn coordinator for wings-level reference, never the failed AI.

📋 Key Takeaways

Pitot tube feeds only the ASI. Static ports feed the ASI, altimeter, and VSI — blocking static affects all three pitot-static instruments.
Pitot blocked with drain open: ASI reads zero. Pitot blocked with drain also blocked: ASI acts like an altimeter (rises in climbs, falls in descents) — the most dangerous scenario.
AI and HI are vacuum-powered; turn coordinator is electric — redundancy by design. Losing vacuum kills AI/HI but not TC. Losing electrical kills TC but not AI/HI.
Compass errors: OSUN (Overshoot South, Undershoot North in turns) and ANDS (Accelerate North, Decelerate South on E/W headings).
Heading indicator precesses — must re-align to compass every 15 minutes in straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight.
Partial panel: use turn coordinator for bank, ASI for pitch trend, altimeter to confirm, and timed turns with the magnetic compass for heading.
