Area III · Task A

Compliance with Air Traffic Control Clearances

References: 14 CFR part 91; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with ATC clearances and procedures while operating solely by reference to instruments.

🎙️ Clearances, Responsibilities, and Void Times IR.III.A.K1

An ATC clearance authorizes you to proceed under specified conditions — it's not a weather briefing, a performance guarantee, or a substitute for your own obstacle/terrain judgment. A clearance with a void time means the clearance is cancelled if you haven't departed by that time, and you must notify ATC by a set deadline if you don't go — otherwise ATC starts treating you as an overdue aircraft that may be off somewhere uncontrolled.

🎙️ Real-World Scenario — Copying a Full Clearance with CRAFT

"Cessna 12345, cleared to Longview airport..."

"Cessna 12345 is cleared to the Longview airport via radar vectors Lufkin, then as filed. Maintain 3,000, expect 7,000 ten minutes after departure. Departure frequency 125.3, squawk 4521."
C — Clearance limit
Longview airport (where this clearance's authorization ends)
R — Route
Radar vectors to Lufkin, then as filed from there
A — Altitude
Maintain 3,000 initially; expect 7,000 ten minutes after departure
F — Frequency
Departure control on 125.3
T — Transponder
Squawk 4521
CRAFT is the standard read-back checklist — working through it in order is how you catch a missing piece (a clearance with no altitude, or a route that doesn't match what you filed) before you're airborne and committed to it.

🚨 PIC Emergency Authority IR.III.A.K2

14 CFR 91.3(b): in an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action, the PIC may deviate from any rule in Part 91 to the extent required to meet that emergency — including departing from an ATC clearance. ATC expects to be told what you did and why (a report may be requested in writing per 91.3(c)), but the authority to act doesn't wait for their permission first.

📻 Lost Communications IR.III.A.K3

14 CFR 91.185 governs IFR flight after a two-way radio failure. If you're in VMC, continue in VMC and land as soon as practicable. If you're in IMC (or VMC becomes IMC), fly the route and altitude below:

R
Route — "AVEF," in priority order: the last Assigned route; if being radar Vectored, direct from the vector termination point to the fix/route/airway specified; the route ATC has told you to Expect in a further clearance; or, lacking any of those, the route Filed in your flight plan.
A
Altitude — fly the HIGHEST of: the altitude in your last ATC clearance; the minimum altitude for IFR operations (MEA, MOCA, etc.) on that route segment; or the altitude ATC has told you to expect in a further clearance.

For timing: if your clearance included an expect-further-clearance (EFC) time, leave your clearance limit at that time. If it's a fix where an approach begins, commence the approach as close as possible to your filed or amended ETA. Squawk 7600 throughout.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.III.A.R1 Partial Understanding

Read back anything you're not 100% sure of rather than guessing — "say again" costs five seconds; flying a wrong clearance costs much more.

IR.III.A.R2 Bad Clearances

ATC is human — an incomplete or inappropriate clearance (an altitude below the MEA, a route through terrain) is something you're expected to catch, not blindly comply with.

IR.III.A.R3 Clearance vs. Capability

A clearance that assumes performance or equipment you don't have (a climb gradient you can't make, a nav system not installed) needs to be challenged before you accept it.

IR.III.A.R4 Similar Call Signs

"November One Two Three Alpha Bravo" and "November One Two Three Bravo Alpha" on frequency together is a real, documented hazard — always include your full call sign and listen for yours specifically, even mid-transmission.

Area III · Task B

Holding Procedures

References: 14 CFR part 91; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with holding procedures solely by reference to instruments.

🔄 Holding Elements IR.III.B.K1

A holding clearance specifies the fix, the direction to hold from it, the leg length or time, the inbound course, the turn direction (right/standard unless otherwise stated), and an expect-further-clearance (EFC) time. Standard holding pattern legs are 1 minute inbound below 14,000 ft MSL and 1.5 minutes above; published holding patterns may specify a leg length in nautical miles (DME holding) instead.

Direct Entry

You're approaching from roughly the same direction as the inbound holding course. Fly to the fix, then turn immediately outbound in the holding direction (standard/right unless stated) and fly the pattern.

Teardrop Entry

You're approaching from nearly the opposite direction, on the holding side. Fly to the fix, continue outbound about 30° offset from the reciprocal of the inbound course (on the holding side) for about a minute, then turn to intercept the inbound course.

Parallel Entry

You're approaching from nearly the opposite direction, on the non-holding side. Fly to the fix, turn to parallel the inbound course outbound on the non-holding side for about a minute, then turn (in the holding direction) back toward the fix to intercept the inbound course.

The One Case You Can Always Recognize

If your track to the fix already matches the published inbound holding course exactly, that's always a direct entry — no sector judgment needed, just fly to the fix and turn outbound.

⛽ Real-World Scenario — The EFC Just Changed

"Expect further clearance at 1715" becomes "1735"

You entered the hold with 90 minutes of fuel on board and a planned 10-minute hold. Twenty minutes in, ATC extends your EFC by another 20 minutes.

1
Recalculate your fuel reserve against the new total hold time, not the original one — this is exactly IR.III.B.R1.
2
If the new numbers eat into your required 45-minute IFR reserve, that's the trigger to advise ATC now — not after you're already below it.
3
Know the difference between "minimum fuel" (no emergency yet, but no room for further delay) and "emergency fuel" (declare now) — and say the word ATC needs to hear, not a vague description.
Holding is the single most common place a routine IFR flight turns into a fuel problem — IR.III.B.R2 and R3 exist because deteriorating destination weather often shows up as exactly this scenario.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.III.B.R1 Recalculating Fuel for an EFC

Every EFC change is a fuel-math checkpoint, not just a new number to read back.

IR.III.B.R2 Minimum Fuel / Emergency

Know the specific phrases ATC needs ("minimum fuel" vs. declaring an emergency) and use them before the situation forces your hand.

IR.III.B.R3 What Leads to Holding

Deteriorating destination weather is the classic cause — build the possibility of a hold into your fuel plan before you ever get the clearance.

IR.III.B.R4 Entry and Wind Correction

Pick your entry before you reach the fix, and correct for wind drift on both legs so your inbound leg actually lines up on the holding course, not just "close."

📋 Key Takeaways

CRAFT — Clearance limit, Route, Altitude, Frequency, Transponder — is the read-back order worth having automatic IR.III.A.K1.
Lost comms: fly route AVEF (Assigned, Vectored, Expected, Filed) and the HIGHEST of assigned/MEA/expected altitude IR.III.A.K3.
PIC emergency authority under 91.3(b) lets you deviate from a clearance first and explain it after, when the emergency genuinely requires it IR.III.A.K2.
A changed EFC time is a fuel-recalculation trigger, not just a new number to read back IR.III.B.R1.
