Day 1

The National Airspace System

Topics: IFH Chapter 8 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe the airspace classes relevant to IFR operations
  • Identify the key symbols on a low-altitude en route chart

🌐 Airspace, briefly, from the IFR seat

ClassWhat it means for an IFR pilot
A18,000 MSL up to FL600. IFR only; no VFR at all.
B, C, DSurround the busier airports. Clearance required to enter Class B; two-way radio contact required for C and D.
EThe default controlled airspace everywhere else it has been designated, and where the large majority of en route IFR flying happens outside terminal areas.
GUncontrolled. This is where the uncontrolled odd/even cruising rule from Week 2 (91.179) would actually apply, though it is rare to be IFR in Class G for long.

🗺️ Reading a low-altitude en route chart IR.I.C.K1e

MEA: Minimum En Route Altitude

Guarantees obstacle clearance and navigation signal reception along the airway segment.

MOCA: Minimum Obstruction Clearance Altitude

Guarantees obstacle clearance only; signal reception is only assured within 22 NM of the VOR (the exception from Week 2's 91.177 lesson).

MCA: Minimum Crossing Altitude

The altitude you must cross a fix at when climbing to a higher MEA immediately after it.

Changeover points

Where you switch from receiving one VOR to the next along an airway, marked so you are always navigating off the stronger, closer signal.

Victor airways

The low-altitude federal airway system, built on VOR radials, depicted as blue lines with a “V” designator (e.g., V163).

📋 Route planning checklist IR.I.C.K1

a
Available navigational facilities along the route: VOR, GPS/RNAV waypoints, and the airway or direct/RNAV routing they support.
b
Special use airspace: MOAs, restricted/prohibited areas, warning areas, and whether they are active for your transit time.
c
Preferred routes published in the Chart Supplement for busy city pairs, which ATC will often assign or expect you to file.
d
Primary and alternate airports: runway lengths, approaches available, fuel and services.
e
Enroute charts (IFR Low/High) for airway structure, MEAs, MOCAs, and reporting points; Chart Supplements for airport data and frequencies.
f
NOTAMs for closures, approach outages, TFRs, and obstruction changes not yet charted; Terminal Procedures for departure, arrival, and approach.
🧮 Worked example

V163: MEA versus MOCA

You are on V163 with a published MEA of 6,000 and a MOCA of 4,000, and you are 12 NM from the VOR that defines the segment.

Per 91.177 you could legally descend to the 4,000 MOCA since you are within 22 NM, but doing so means you may lose reliable VOR reception even though obstacle clearance is fine. MEA is the altitude that guarantees both.
Self-check
  1. What does MEA guarantee that MOCA does not?
  2. What is an MCA, and when does it apply?
  3. Is Class A airspace ever available to a VFR pilot?
  4. What color and label identifies a Victor airway on a low-altitude en route chart?
Show answers
  1. Navigation signal reception along the entire segment, not just obstacle clearance.
  2. A Minimum Crossing Altitude: the altitude you must cross a specific fix at when climbing to a higher MEA that begins at or after that fix.
  3. No. Class A, 18,000 MSL through FL600, is IFR-only.
  4. A blue line labeled with a “V” and a number, e.g., V163.

Day 2

The Air Traffic Control System

Topics: IFH Chapter 9, AIM Chapter 5 (skim) · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe the ATC facilities involved in a typical IFR flight from clearance to landing
  • State the pilot's and controller's respective responsibilities under an IFR clearance

📡 Who you talk to, in order IR.V.B.K2

1
Clearance Delivery (or tower/Flight Service if there is no dedicated clearance frequency)
2
Ground
3
Tower
4
Departure (a TRACON, Terminal Radar Approach Control)
5
Center (the Air Route Traffic Control Center, ARTCC) for the en route portion
6
Approach (again a TRACON), then Tower at the destination

Center hands you between sectors as you cross their boundaries; you will not always notice the transition beyond a frequency change.

⚖️ Pilot and controller responsibilities IR.III.A.K1

ATC's job is separation, sequencing, and safety alerts; the pilot-in-command's job is compliance with the clearance, and final authority for the safe operation of the flight. If a clearance would compromise safety, you can decline it and request an amendment; if you deviate from a clearance in an emergency, you are expected to notify ATC as soon as possible. “Cleared” from ATC is not a suggestion, but it is also not a substitute for your own judgment about terrain, weather, or aircraft capability. Under 91.3(b), in an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action the PIC may deviate from any Part 91 rule to the extent the emergency requires IR.III.A.K2.

🎙️ Real-World Scenario — Copying a Full Clearance with CRAFT

“Cessna 12345, cleared to Longview airport…”

“Cessna 12345 is cleared to the Longview airport via radar vectors Lufkin, then as filed. Maintain 3,000, expect 7,000 ten minutes after departure. Departure frequency 125.3, squawk 4521.”

LetterElementIn this clearance
CClearance limitLongview airport
RRouteRadar vectors to Lufkin, then as filed
AAltitudeMaintain 3,000; expect 7,000 ten minutes after departure
FFrequencyDeparture on 125.3
TTransponderSquawk 4521
CRAFT is the standard read-back order. Working through it is how you catch a missing piece (a clearance with no altitude, or a route that does not match what you filed) before you are airborne. A clearance with a void time is cancelled if you have not departed by then, and you must notify ATC by a set deadline if you do not go.

Partial understanding IR.III.A.R1

Read back anything you are not sure of. “Say again” costs five seconds; flying a wrong clearance costs much more.

Similar call signs IR.III.A.R4

Always use your full call sign and listen for yours specifically, even mid-transmission.

🧭 Worked example

A clearance you can decline

You are cleared direct to a fix that would take you through a buildup you can see on your onboard weather display.

You are not required to accept that clearance as-is; request a deviation or an alternate routing. Compliance with a clearance never overrides your authority (and responsibility) as PIC to avoid known hazards.
Self-check
  1. Name the ATC facility that typically handles the en route portion of an IFR flight between departure and destination terminal areas.
  2. If an ATC clearance would compromise safety, what is the pilot's option?
  3. What is expected of a pilot who deviates from a clearance in an emergency?
Show answers
  1. The Air Route Traffic Control Center (ARTCC), or Center.
  2. Decline it and request an amended clearance; the PIC retains final authority for the safe operation of the flight.
  3. Notify ATC of the deviation as soon as possible.
