Day 1

Flight Planning and Filing an IFR Flight Plan

Topics: IFH Chapter 10 (first half) · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Name the main sources of preflight planning information for an IFR flight
  • Identify the required fields on an IFR flight plan

🗂️ Where the planning information comes from IR.I.C.K1

NOTAMs (including TFRs), the Chart Supplement for airport and frequency data, current and forecast weather (Weeks 5–8), and the route structure (en route charts, preferred IFR routes, published departures and arrivals) all feed the preflight briefing IR.I.C.K1g IR.I.C.K1f.

Briefing typeWhat it is
StandardThe full picture: adverse conditions, synopsis, current conditions, en route forecast, destination forecast, winds aloft, NOTAMs, and ATC delays.
AbbreviatedOnly what you ask for, used to supplement information you already have.
OutlookFor a flight 6 or more hours away; a general look at expected trends.

✍️ Filing the flight plan IR.I.C.K4

FieldNotes
Aircraft identification and typePlus the equipment suffix (for example /G for GPS with transponder and Mode C, /A for DME, /U for transponder with Mode C but no DME).
True airspeedCruise TAS, not indicated.
Departure point and timeProposed departure time in UTC.
Cruising altitude and routeBelow 18,000 ft in uncontrolled airspace, IFR altitudes follow the odd/even rule: odd thousands for magnetic courses 0–179, even thousands for 180–359 (91.179).
Destination and estimated time en route
AlternateIf required (Week 2: the 1-2-3 rule, 91.169).
Fuel on boardIn hours and minutes.
Pilot and passenger informationPilot name and contact, number aboard, aircraft color.

The equipment suffix tells ATC what navigation and approach capability you actually have (for example a WAAS GPS versus a basic VOR/DME setup), which affects the routes and approaches you can be cleared for. File at least 30 minutes before you want to depart; in the air, file early enough that ATC can process it before the point where you need the clearance.

Opening and closing IR.I.C.K5
At a towered airport the IFR flight plan closes automatically when you land. At a non-towered airport you must cancel it, by radio or by telephone to Flight Service, or ATC will start searching for you. If a clearance has a void time and you are not airborne by then, the clearance is void, and you must tell ATC your intentions within 30 minutes of the void time IR.III.A.K1.
🧭 Worked example

A perfectly filed plan through a TFR

You file for 8,000 ft on a route that crosses a TFR you did not check.

A correctly filed flight plan does not protect you from a TFR violation. Reviewing NOTAMs, including TFRs, is part of the required preflight action under 91.103, separate from and in addition to filing the flight plan itself.
Self-check
  1. Name three components of a standard weather briefing beyond just the forecast.
  2. What does the equipment suffix on an IFR flight plan tell ATC?
  3. At a non-towered airport, who closes your IFR flight plan?
Show answers
  1. Any three of: adverse conditions, synopsis, current conditions, winds aloft, NOTAMs, ATC delays.
  2. What navigation and approach capability the aircraft actually has (for example WAAS GPS versus basic VOR/DME), which affects what routes and approaches can be assigned.
  3. You do, by radio or telephone. It closes automatically only at a towered airport.

Day 2

Departure Procedures: ODP vs. SID

Topics: IPH Chapter 1 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Distinguish an ODP from a SID and state whether each requires an ATC clearance

⛰️ ODP: obstacle clearance, not efficiency IR.V.B.K1

An Obstacle Departure Procedure exists purely to provide obstacle clearance, via the least restrictive route out of the terminal area. ODPs may be textual or graphic (graphic ones carry “(OBSTACLE)” in the title). You may fly a published ODP without an ATC clearance unless ATC assigns something else (a SID or radar vectors). A textual ODP cannot be filed as part of your route; a graphic ODP, having its own identifier, can.

✈️ SID: efficiency, and it does require a clearance

A Standard Instrument Departure is an ATC procedure, always published graphically, designed to reduce pilot/controller workload and speed traffic flow at busier airports. You must receive a clearance that includes the SID before flying it. Verify your aircraft can meet any published climb gradient before accepting one; if it cannot, request “no SID” or tell ATC. Being cleared via a SID satisfies any ODP for that runway, but being cleared for an ODP does not clear you for the SID.

📐 Climb gradients

Departure procedures assume a standard climb of 200 ft per NM unless a steeper gradient is published. Convert a gradient to a rate of climb: feet per minute = gradient (ft/NM) × groundspeed (kt) ÷ 60.

Published gradientGroundspeedRequired climb rate
300 ft/NM90 kt300 × 90 ÷ 60 = 450 fpm
400 ft/NM100 kt400 × 100 ÷ 60 ≈ 670 fpm

🛬 STARs

Standard Terminal Arrival Routes are the arrival-side counterpart: published transitions into the terminal area that may carry altitude and speed constraints. Accept a STAR only if you can comply with its constraints, and know what a “descend via” clearance authorizes before you hear it in the air IR.V.B.K1.

🧭 Worked example

“Cleared as filed, climb and maintain 4,000”

A graphic ODP marked “(OBSTACLE)” is published for your departure runway, and ATC's clearance says nothing about it.

You can fly the ODP on your own initiative for obstacle protection, since nothing overrides it. If ATC instead assigns a SID or starts vectoring you right after departure, that assignment takes precedence and ATC becomes responsible for your obstacle and terrain separation under the vector.
Self-check
  1. Do you need an ATC clearance to fly a published ODP, absent any other instruction?
  2. Do you need an ATC clearance to fly a SID?
  3. Can a textual ODP be filed as part of your route in the flight plan?
Show answers
  1. No.
  2. Yes, the clearance must specifically include it.
  3. No; only a graphic ODP, which has its own identifier, can be filed.

Day 3

En Route Procedures and Lost-Comm Re-Drill

Topics: IPH Chapter 2 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe standard en route reporting and rerouting procedures
  • Re-apply Week 2's 91.185 lost-comm logic against real charted routes

🧭 En route, briefly IR.V.B.K2

Once established en route, your jobs are to follow your cleared route and altitude, monitor the assigned frequency, make any required reports (91.183), and stay ahead of reroutes, holds, and altitude changes ATC may issue for traffic or weather. If you ever find yourself off the published MEA structure, apply the minimum IFR altitude rules from Week 2 (91.177).

Reports ATC expects (91.183, 91.187, AIM 5-3-3)
At all times: vacating an assigned altitude; being unable to climb or descend at least 500 fpm; a missed approach; a change in average true airspeed of 5% or 10 knots (whichever is greater) from your filed value; and the time and altitude of reaching and leaving a holding fix (these two may be omitted in radar contact). Always: unforecast weather, any malfunction of navigation, approach, or communication equipment (91.187), and anything else affecting safety of flight (91.183). Outside radar contact you also make position reports at compulsory reporting points.

🗻 Chart altitudes at a glance

AltitudeWhat it guarantees

📻 Lost communications IR.III.A.K3 IR.VII.A.K1

Squawk 7600 and try your backup radio and frequencies first. In VFR conditions, continue VFR and land as soon as practicable. In IMC or when you cannot maintain VFR, 91.185 gives you the route and altitude:

Route: AVEF, the first that appliesAltitude: the highest of...
1AssignedThe altitude assigned in the last clearance
2Vectored (go direct to the fix or route in the vector clearance)The minimum IFR altitude (MEA) for the segment being flown
3Expected (in a further clearance)The altitude ATC told you to expect
4Filed

If your clearance limit is a fix from which an approach begins, begin descent and approach as close as possible to the EFC time (or the flight-plan ETA if none was given).

🧭 Worked example

Lost comm on real numbers

Pull up a real low-altitude en route chart for a route you might fly and note the MEAs along it. Re-run Week 2's lost-comm scenario with those numbers instead of made-up ones.

If comms failed right now, what altitude would you be required to fly on the segment you are on, and what would you fly once you crossed onto a segment with a higher MEA? The altitude rule is evaluated for the segment being flown, so a higher MEA ahead becomes your required altitude when you reach it.
Self-check
  1. What regulation governs the routine position and weather reports you make en route?
  2. If you lost comms right now on a segment with a higher MEA ahead, what altitude rule would apply once you crossed onto that segment?
Show answers
  1. 91.183 (Week 2).
  2. You would fly the highest of your last assigned altitude, that segment's minimum IFR altitude, or any altitude ATC said to expect, per 91.185.
