Day 1

Pitot-Static System and Errors

Topics: IFH Chapter 3 (pitot-static) · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • Explain how the altimeter, airspeed indicator, and VSI derive their readings
  • Diagnose the three classic pitot-static blockage scenarios

🔧 One system, three instruments IR.II.B.K1a

The altimeter and VSI use static pressure only. The airspeed indicator compares ram (pitot) pressure against static pressure, so airspeed is really a measurement of the difference between the two. That shared plumbing is why one blockage can produce very different symptoms depending on which port is blocked and whether the pitot drain hole is involved.

InstrumentPressure sourceNote
AltimeterStaticAneroid wafers expand and contract with static pressure.
VSIStaticUses a calibrated leak, so it lags by several seconds; read it as a trend.
Airspeed indicatorPitot (ram) minus staticReads the pressure difference.

🚧 The three classic failures IR.II.B.R3

FailureAltimeterVSIAirspeed indicator
Pitot blocked, drain hole openNormalNormalDrops to zero (or near it) regardless of actual speed: the trapped ram air bleeds out the drain hole.
Pitot blocked, drain hole also blockedNormalNormalActs like an altimeter. Pressure is trapped, so it reads higher as you climb and lower as you descend, and does not respond to airspeed changes.
Static port blocked, pitot clearFreezes at the altitude of the blockageReads zeroWrong in the opposite sense: reads lower than actual when you climb above the blockage altitude, higher than actual when you descend below it.
Why the static-blocked ASI behaves that way
The trapped static pressure is the value at the blockage altitude. Climb: real static pressure drops, but the trapped pressure stays higher, so the pitot-minus-static difference shrinks and the ASI under-reads. Descend: the trapped value is lower than the real static pressure, so the difference grows and the ASI over-reads.

Many aircraft carry an alternate static source for exactly this failure. It usually reads slightly differently from the primary because of installation error, so check the POH for the corrections.

🧭 Worked example

The frozen altimeter

In cruise you notice the altimeter has not moved in several minutes despite a descent you can feel, the VSI has gone to zero, and the airspeed is reading noticeably fast for the descent attitude you are holding.

Frozen altimeter, zeroed VSI, and an ASI reading high in a descent is a blocked static port. Switch to the alternate static source if installed, and reinterpret the ASI and altimeter with the POH corrections. Try the interactive pitot-static failure simulator in the archived Instruments module to see each combination.
Self-check
  1. Pitot tube blocked, drain hole open. What does the ASI read?
  2. Pitot tube and drain hole both blocked, then you climb. What happens to the ASI reading?
  3. Static port blocked, pitot clear. What happens to the altimeter and VSI?
  4. With a blocked static port, what happens to the ASI reading if you climb above the altitude where the blockage occurred?
Show answers
  1. Zero, or close to it, regardless of actual airspeed.
  2. It reads progressively higher than actual airspeed as you climb.
  3. The altimeter freezes at the altitude of the blockage; the VSI reads zero continuously.
  4. It reads lower than actual airspeed (and higher than actual if you descend below the blockage altitude).

The Magnetic Compass and Its Errors

Topics: IFH Chapter 3 (compass) · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • State the acceleration and turning error mnemonics and when each applies
  • Explain why these errors are worst on certain headings and negligible on others

🧭 Two errors, two mnemonics IR.II.B.K1d

Acceleration error: ANDSTurning error: UNOS
RuleAccelerate North, Decelerate SouthUndershoot North, Overshoot South
WhenChanging airspeed on an easterly or westerly heading (Northern Hemisphere)Turning through a northerly or southerly heading (Northern Hemisphere)
EffectAccelerating swings the compass toward north; decelerating, toward southThrough north the compass lags (shows less turn than actual); through south it leads (shows more turn than actual)
Worst onEast and west headingsNorth and south headings
Zero onNorth and south headingsEast and west headings

Both errors come from magnetic dip acting on the pivoting compass card, which is why they are opposite in where they hurt: one needs a change in speed, the other a change in heading.

🧭 Worked example

Rolling out on north by compass alone

You are turning to a heading of 360 using only the magnetic compass. Because the compass lags through north, it is still showing less turn than you have actually made.

Roll out before the compass reads 360. A common rule of thumb is to lead by roughly your latitude (Austin is near 30°N). Turning to a southerly heading, the compass leads, so you hold the turn past the indication by a similar amount.
Self-check
  1. On a westerly heading, you accelerate. Which way does the compass momentarily swing?
  2. Turning through south, does the compass lead or lag the actual turn?
  3. On which headings is acceleration error essentially zero?
  4. On which headings is turning error essentially zero?
Show answers
  1. Toward north (ANDS: Accelerate North).
  2. It leads (overshoots): Overshoot South.
  3. North and south headings.
  4. East and west headings.

Gyroscopic Instruments and Power Sources

Topics: IFH Chapter 3 (gyros, HSI) · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • Name the gyroscopic instruments and their typical power sources
  • Explain what an HSI adds over a separate heading indicator and CDI

🌀 The gyroscopic instruments IR.II.B.K1b

InstrumentShowsTypical power (traditional GA single)Behavior to know
Attitude indicatorPitch and bankVacuum (electric and AHRS-based versions are now common)When power is lost it spins down slowly and gives subtly wrong information rather than failing obviously.
Heading indicatorHeadingVacuumNo magnetic reference of its own. It precesses (drifts), so reset it against the magnetic compass periodically.
Turn coordinatorRate of turn and roll rateElectricElectric on purpose: it survives a vacuum failure.
Why the mixed layout
Pairing a vacuum attitude indicator and heading indicator with an electric turn coordinator means a single failed system (vacuum pump or the electrical bus) does not take out every gyro at once. Know your own aircraft's actual power sources, since glass-panel aircraft usually run everything from redundant electrical sources instead IR.II.B.K1c.

🖥️ HSI: one instrument, less cross-check

A Horizontal Situation Indicator combines the heading indicator with a VOR/localizer course deviation display, so heading and course deviation are read from one place instead of cross-checking a separate heading indicator and CDI. That reduces scan workload, which matters most on a busy approach.

🧭 Worked example

Vacuum failure in cruise

In an aircraft with a vacuum attitude indicator and heading indicator and an electric turn coordinator, you lose vacuum pressure.

Expect the attitude indicator to slowly and subtly give false pitch/bank information as it spins down, and the heading indicator to drift. The turn coordinator, being electric, stays reliable and becomes your primary bank reference for partial-panel flying (Week 15). IR.II.B.R3 is this: knowing which instruments fail together.
Self-check
  1. In a traditional GA light single, which instrument is typically electric specifically so it survives a vacuum failure?
  2. Why does the heading indicator need periodic resetting against the magnetic compass?
  3. What does an HSI combine into one instrument?
Show answers
  1. The turn coordinator.
  2. It has no magnetic reference of its own and is subject to precession, so it drifts over time.
  3. The heading indicator and a VOR/localizer course deviation display.
