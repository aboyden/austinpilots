Day 1

61.65 Requirements and the ACS Roadmap

Topics: 14 CFR 61.65, Instrument Rating ACS · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • State the aeronautical knowledge and experience requirements for the instrument-airplane rating
  • Explain how the ACS defines what is tested and how to use it as a study map

📜 61.65: what the rating actually requires IR.I.A.K1

To hold an instrument-airplane rating you need all of the following:

1
A current private pilot certificate (or concurrent application) with an appropriate category rating.
2
English language proficiency.
3
Logged ground training or a home-study course on the 61.65(b) knowledge areas.
4
An instructor endorsement certifying you are prepared for the knowledge test (61.65(a)(4)).
5
Logged training on the areas of operation in 61.65(c).
6
A separate instructor endorsement certifying you are prepared for the practical test.
7
A passing score on the knowledge test.

For the instrument-airplane rating specifically, 61.65(d) requires 50 hours of cross-country time as pilot in command, at least 10 of which must be in airplanes, plus 40 hours of actual or simulated instrument time on the areas of operation in 61.65(c). That experience matters more once flight training starts; for the knowledge test itself, the endorsement in 61.65(a)(4) is the gate that actually matters right now.

🗺️ The ACS as your syllabus

The Instrument Rating ACS lists Areas of Operation, each broken into Tasks with specific Knowledge, Risk Management, and Skill elements. The knowledge test draws from the Knowledge elements across these areas. Reading the ACS front to back will not give you exact questions, but it tells you exactly what is in scope, so nothing about the breadth of the test is a surprise later. Throughout this course, items are tagged with their ACS element codes (IR.I.A.K1 for Knowledge, IR.I.A.R1 for Risk Management) so you can trace each fact back to the standard.

🔄 Beyond the test: keeping the rating current (61.57)

Earning the rating and staying current are different things. The knowledge test will not ask you to fly the approaches, but you will meet the currency rules in the regs and in checkride orals, so it is worth knowing the shape of them now.

Instrument currency: 6 approaches in 6 months

To act as PIC under IFR, within the preceding 6 calendar months you need 6 instrument approaches, holding procedures and tasks, and intercepting/tracking courses using navigation systems (61.57(c)). These can be flown in the aircraft, a flight simulator, or an approved training device.

Lapsed: the 6/6/12 window

Miss the 6 months and you have 6 more months to regain currency with a safety pilot where one is required. Past 12 months total, you need an Instrument Proficiency Check with a CFII, examiner, or other authorized person (61.57(d)).

🧭 Real-World Scenario — The Lapsed Pilot

Maria's currency clock

Maria flew her 6th approach of the cycle on March 1, then got busy and flew no more approaches.

Thru Sep 1
Month 6: fully current. Any IFR flight is legal.
Sep–Mar
Months 7–12: lapsed but inside the 6/6/12 window. She can fly 6 approaches, holding, and tracking with a safety pilot and be legal again the moment she finishes.
After Mar 1
Past month 12: the safety-pilot option is gone. She needs an Instrument Proficiency Check, however close she was to finishing on her own.
The lesson: the 6-month and 12-month marks are two different sets of rules, not one deadline with rising urgency. IR.I.A.R1 (proficiency versus currency) is the judgment side of the same topic.

  3. What must an instructor endorse before you can schedule the knowledge test?
  4. What document effectively serves as the FAA's syllabus for what is tested on the knowledge test?

  2. 40 hours, on the areas of operation listed in 61.65(c).
  3. A logbook or training record endorsement certifying you are prepared for the knowledge test, based on ground training in the 61.65(b) knowledge areas.
  4. The Instrument Rating Airman Certification Standards (ACS).

Sensory Systems and Illusions

Topics: IFH Chapter 1 (first half) · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Explain why the vestibular and visual systems can mislead a pilot with no outside visual reference
  • Name at least five specific spatial-disorientation illusions and their triggers

🧠 Why your body lies to you in the clouds IR.IV.A.R2

The inner ear's semicircular canals sense rotation and the otolith organs sense linear acceleration and gravity, but neither can tell the difference between gravity and other forms of acceleration, and the canals stop signaling a turn once it becomes constant-rate. With no outside horizon to check against, the brain fills the gap with a confident but wrong sensation. Vision is the dominant sense for orientation; take it away (or give it a false horizon) and the vestibular and proprioceptive systems take over, and they are not built for this job.

In practice
The inner ear is convincing in IMC. A sustained turn can feel like straight flight, and rolling level afterward can feel like a turn the other way. Fatigue, illness, and even a stuffy nose degrade your scan before you necessarily feel impaired IR.IV.A.R1.

🌀 The illusions worth memorizing

IllusionTriggerWhat it does to you
The leansA slow, subtle roll into a bank goes unnoticedA sudden correction back to level feels like banking the other way.
Coriolis illusionAn abrupt head movement during a prolonged turnStimulates a different canal pair: a false sense of rotation or tumbling on a different axis. Considered one of the most dangerous.
Graveyard spiralA prolonged, constant-rate turnThe turn feels like it has stopped; leveling feels like a turn the other way, so the pilot re-enters the turn (often pulling back), tightening the spiral.
Somatogravic illusionRapid acceleration (takeoff, go-around)Feels like a nose-up pitch, prompting an unwarranted nose-down correction. Rapid deceleration gives the opposite sensation.
Inversion illusionAbrupt change from climb to straight-and-levelA tumbling-backward sensation.
Elevator illusionAbrupt vertical acceleration (updraft or downdraft)An updraft feels like nose-up pitch; a downdraft feels like nose-down.
False horizonSloping cloud decks, dark scenes with ground lights, stars mistaken for lightsMisreading something else as the horizon.
AutokinesisStaring at one stationary light in the dark for several secondsThe light appears to move on its own.
🌀 Worked example

Coriolis in the cockpit

In IMC, established in a shallow turn, you turn your head to check a chart on the side console. You suddenly feel like the aircraft is tumbling on an axis that does not match anything you are actually doing.

That is the Coriolis illusion, triggered by the head movement during the turn. The fix is the same for nearly all of these: trust the instruments, keep head movements smooth and minimal during maneuvering, and do not try to out-feel the panel.
Self-check
  1. Which illusion is triggered specifically by an abrupt head movement during a prolonged turn?
  2. A pilot in a prolonged, constant-rate turn feels like the turn has stopped, levels the wings, and unknowingly re-enters the original turn while pulling back on the yoke. What is this, and why is it dangerous?
  3. Which illusion is caused by a rapid acceleration on takeoff or go-around, and what false correction does it tend to provoke?
  4. What is autokinesis, and in what condition does it occur?
Show answers
  1. The Coriolis illusion.
  2. The graveyard spiral; pulling back tightens the turn and can lead to a steepening spiral dive without the pilot realizing it.
  3. The somatogravic illusion; it feels like an excessive nose-up pitch, prompting an unwarranted nose-down correction.
  4. A stationary light in the dark appears to move after being stared at for several seconds; it occurs at night with a single point-source light and no other visual reference.

Getting Set Up

Topics: administrative setup · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Complete the administrative steps that everything else in this plan depends on

🧰 Why each of these matters

IACRA account and FTN

PSI (the FAA's testing vendor) will not let you schedule the knowledge test without an FAA Tracking Number, and getting one requires an IACRA account. Do this now so it is not a surprise when you are ready to test in Month 4.

CFII contact

You need an instructor endorsement before you can sit the knowledge test (61.65(a)(4)), so line one up now for periodic ground briefings, even though most of this plan is self-directed study.

FAR/AIM and ACS

Both are free PDFs from faa.gov and are the source documents this whole plan reads from.

Missed-question log

One running note or spreadsheet for every practice question you get wrong across all 17 weeks. This is the single highest-leverage habit in the plan; you will revisit it at every checkpoint.

Question bank

Sheppard Air or Gleim, roughly $50–70, is the paid piece of this frugal stack. Buy and activate it now so Week 2's daily question sets are not blocked.

Self-check

  2. Why do you need a CFII lined up now, even if most of your study is self-directed?
  3. What is the single habit this plan asks you to maintain across all 17 weeks, starting now?
Show answers
  1. An FAA Tracking Number (FTN), obtained through an IACRA account.
  2. You need an instructor endorsement (61.65(a)(4)) certifying you are prepared for the knowledge test before you can sit it.
  3. A running missed-question log, reviewed at every checkpoint.
