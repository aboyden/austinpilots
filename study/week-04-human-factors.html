Day 1

Physiological and Medical Factors

Topics: IFH Chapter 1 (remainder), 14 CFR 91.17 · About 70 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe hypoxia, hyperventilation, and other physiological threats specific to instrument flight
  • State the 91.17 alcohol and drug rules from memory

🫁 Physiological factors IR.I.A.R3

Hypoxia

Insufficient oxygen to the brain and tissues. The big one at altitude, and dangerous because early symptoms (euphoria, poor judgment, tunnel vision, cyanosis) rarely feel alarming to the person experiencing them. Supplemental oxygen and a pulse oximeter are cheap insurance above the altitudes where you would notice the effects.

Hyperventilation

Ironically produces very similar symptoms (dizziness, tingling, visual disturbance) from over-breathing, usually from stress. The fix is slow, controlled breathing, or talking, the opposite instinct from what hypoxia calls for, so correctly telling the two apart matters.

Before every flight: IMSAFE
Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion. 14 CFR 61.53 prohibits acting as PIC with a known medical deficiency or while using medication that affects your faculties in a way contrary to safety, and that applies under BasicMed too IR.IV.A.R1.

🍺 91.17: alcohol and drugs

8 hr
No person may act as a crewmember within 8 hours of consuming alcohol.
BAC
…or with a blood alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater.
Imp.
…or while under the influence, or while using a drug that affects safety.

Many operators and personal minimums use a stricter 12- or 24-hour rule. “8 hours bottle to throttle” is the regulatory floor, not a target.

🫁 Worked example

The pleasant kind of problem

At 9,000 ft cabin altitude with no supplemental oxygen after several hours, a pilot feels unusually good and slightly euphoric, and starts making small navigation errors without noticing.

That euphoric, false sense of competence is a classic early hypoxia presentation, not a sign that things are going well.
Self-check
  1. Why is hypoxia particularly dangerous compared to other physiological threats?
  2. What blood alcohol concentration makes it illegal to act as a crewmember under 91.17?
  3. What is the minimum time after consuming alcohol before acting as a crewmember under 91.17?
Show answers
  1. Early symptoms (euphoria, impaired judgment) often feel pleasant or unremarkable to the person experiencing them, so self-recognition is unreliable.
  2. 0.04 or greater.
  3. 8 hours.

Aeronautical Decision Making and Hazardous Attitudes

Topics: IFH Chapter 1 (ADM, CRM) · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Name the five classic hazardous attitudes and their antidotes
  • Describe how single-pilot resource management applies without a crew

⚠️ The five hazardous attitudes

AttitudeThe thinkingAntidote
Anti-authority“Don't tell me.”Follow the rules; they are usually right.
Impulsivity“Do something now.”Not so fast. Think first.
Invulnerability“It won't happen to me.”It could happen to me.
Macho“I can do it.”Taking chances is foolish.
Resignation“What's the use?”I'm not helpless. I can make a difference.

🧑‍✈️ CRM without a crew

Single-Pilot Resource Management means using every resource available: ATC, the autopilot, checklists, passengers who can hold a chart, and, critically, your own workload management. The goal is to recognize when task saturation is building and offload something (engage the autopilot, ask ATC for a delay vector, defer a nonessential task) before it costs you situational awareness. Stress, task saturation, and an inadequate scan are exactly the precursors the ACS names for loss of control in flight IR.IV.B.R1.

A simple frame: PAVE
Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures. It organizes the risk categories that show up across nearly every ACS task.
Where human factors live in the ACS
The current ACS has no separate human-factors task. Instead the topics appear as Risk Management elements spread through the tasks, for example personal minimums IR.I.A.R2, fitness for flight IR.I.A.R3, and spatial disorientation IR.IV.A.R2.
😤 Worked example

Pressing on

A pilot behind schedule, in deteriorating weather, presses on toward the original destination rather than diverting to a closer airport with better conditions, reasoning “I've flown in worse.”

That is the macho attitude talking. The antidote is recognizing that taking the chance buys nothing but risk.
Self-check
  1. What is the antidote to the “invulnerability” hazardous attitude?
  2. A pilot reacts to a minor abnormal indication by immediately pulling circuit breakers without diagnosing the problem first. Which hazardous attitude does this resemble, and what is the antidote?
  3. In single-pilot IFR, what is the core idea behind resource management when workload is climbing?
Show answers
  1. “It could happen to me.”
  2. Impulsivity; the antidote is “not so fast, think first.”
  3. Recognize task saturation building and offload something (autopilot, ATC assistance, deferring a nonessential task) before situational awareness suffers.

Self-check
  1. Weighted toward regulations, the IFR system, and human factors: what is the 1-2-3 rule?
  2. What altitude do you fly in controlled airspace under an IFR clearance, regardless of magnetic course?
  3. Which illusion is triggered by an abrupt head movement during a prolonged turn?
  4. What is the antidote to the macho hazardous attitude?
  5. What must be true before you can go below DA/MDA on an approach?
Show answers
  1. An alternate is required unless the destination forecast, 1 hour before to 1 hour after ETA, shows a ceiling at or above 2,000 ft and visibility at or above 3 SM for the whole window.
  2. Whatever altitude or flight level ATC assigned.
  3. The Coriolis illusion.
  4. “Taking chances is foolish.”
  5. You are continuously positioned for a normal landing, flight visibility meets the published minimum, and at least one qualifying visual reference for the runway is distinctly visible and identifiable.
