Day 1

Holding Pattern Entries and Timing

Topics: AIM 5-3-8 · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • Choose the correct entry (direct, parallel, teardrop) from a given inbound heading
  • State the standard holding timing and maximum holding speeds

🔁 Choosing the entry IR.III.B.K1

A standard hold uses right turns; a nonstandard hold uses left turns. Picture the holding course extended through the fix, with a second line through the fix at 70° to the holding course. Together they split the plane into three sectors: direct (180° wide, on the holding-pattern side of the 70° line), and, across the 70° line, a narrow teardrop sector (70°, on the holding side) and a wider parallel sector (110°, on the non-holding side).

Standard (right-turn) hold, inbound course 360°. By arrival heading at the fix: teardrop 180°–250°, parallel 070°–180°, direct 250° through 360° to 070°.

🧮 The quick way: arrival heading relative to the inbound course

Compute r = (your heading − inbound course), adding 360 if negative. For a standard (right-turn) hold:

r (degrees)Entry
250 up through 360 and on to 070Direct
070 to 180Parallel
180 to 250Teardrop

For a left-turn hold the picture is mirrored: use r = (inbound course − your heading) with the same ranges. Near a boundary, either neighboring entry is acceptable; do not agonize, just be stable on the pattern.

🛩️ How each entry is flown

Direct
Cross the fix and turn to the outbound heading, in the direction of the hold.
Teardrop
Cross the fix and turn to a heading 30° off the outbound heading, on the holding side. Fly it for the standard time, then turn in the direction of the hold to intercept the inbound course.
Parallel
Cross the fix and turn to a heading parallel to the holding course, outbound, on the non-holding side. After the standard time, turn in the direction of the holding pattern through more than 180° and return to the fix or intercept the inbound course.

⏱️ Timing and speed

Standard legs are timed to give a 1-minute inbound leg at or below 14,000 ft MSL and 1.5 minutes above 14,000 ft. The first outbound leg uses the same time (timing starts abeam the fix, or when wings level after the turn, whichever is later); later outbound legs are adjusted for wind so the inbound leg takes the standard time. A clearance may specify a DME or RNAV leg length instead. Turns are standard rate. Correct for wind by applying roughly double the inbound drift correction on the outbound leg.

Altitude (MSL)Maximum holding airspeed (civil, US)
Up to 6,000 ft200 KIAS
6,001 to 14,000 ft230 KIAS
Above 14,000 ft265 KIAS

Some published holds restrict speed further. Slow to holding speed when you are 3 minutes or less from the fix, but before reaching it IR.III.B.S2. A holding clearance gives you the fix, the direction from the fix, the radial or course, the leg length if DME-based, left turns if nonstandard, and an expect-further-clearance (EFC) time.

Fuel and the EFC time IR.III.B.R1
If you are given an unexpected EFC time, recalculate your fuel reserve immediately. Holding is where a comfortable fuel margin quietly disappears.
🧭 Worked example

Hold NE of the fix on the 045 radial, right turns

The inbound course is 225 and the outbound heading is 045. You arrive at the fix on a heading of 100.

r = 100 − 225 = −125, plus 360 = 235.

235 falls in the 180 to 250 range, so this is a teardrop entry. Cross the fix and turn to a heading 30° off the outbound heading toward the holding side (045 − 30 = 015), fly it for the standard time, then turn right to intercept the 225 inbound course.
Self-check
  1. What is the standard inbound leg time at or below 14,000 ft MSL, and above it?
  2. Which entry has you cross the fix and turn to a heading about 30 degrees off the outbound leg before turning again to intercept inbound?
  3. What is the maximum holding airspeed from 6,001 to 14,000 ft MSL for civil aircraft in the US?
  4. Right-turn hold, inbound course 180. You arrive on a heading of 270. Which entry?
  5. Right-turn hold, inbound course 090. You arrive on a heading of 090. Which entry?
Show answers
  1. 1 minute at or below 14,000 ft MSL, 1.5 minutes above 14,000 ft MSL.
  2. Teardrop entry.
  3. 230 KIAS.
  4. r = 270 − 180 = 90, which falls in 070 to 180: parallel.
  5. r = 0: direct.

Day 2

Course Intercepts

Topics: Intercept heading problems · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Compute an intercept heading to join a course from a given position

🎯 The basic intercept method IR.V.A.K1

To join a course you are not on, turn to a heading that creates an intercept angle between your heading and the desired course, typically 30° to 45°. Hold it until the CDI shows you approaching the course, then turn to the course heading and refine. Use a shallower angle close in (to avoid blowing through) and a steeper one farther out for a quicker join.

1
Set the OBS (or HSI course) to the course you want, oriented so it points the way you intend to fly.
2
Note which way the needle is deflected: the course is on that side of you.
3
Turn toward the needle. Intercept heading = course ± the intercept angle, toward the needle side.
4
As the needle starts to move toward center, turn to the course heading, apply a wind correction, and bracket in small steps (for example 10° then 5°).
ACS tolerances IR.V.A.K1
Hold the selected heading within ±5°, altitude within ±100 ft, and allow no more than three-quarter-scale CDI deflection while tracking (IR.V.A.S5, S6).
🧭 Worked example

Joining the 090 radial inbound

You want to join the 090 radial inbound, so the course to fly is 270. You are south of that line on a heading of 300 with the CDI at full-scale deflection to the right.

Flying west, the course line is on your right, so the needle is on the correct side to turn toward. A 45° intercept means heading 270 + 45 = 315.

Heading 300 is already a 30° intercept and would work. Whichever you choose, hold it until the needle starts moving, then shallow the angle as you close so you do not overshoot the course.
Self-check
  1. What is a typical intercept angle for a moderate course join?
  2. Should the intercept angle be steeper or shallower when you are close to the course versus far from it?
Show answers
  1. 30 to 45 degrees.
  2. Shallower close in (to avoid overshooting); steeper is acceptable farther out for a quicker join.

Self-check
  1. Pitot blocked, drain hole blocked too. You climb. What does the ASI do?
  2. On a northerly heading, does turning error cause the compass to lead or lag the actual turn?
  3. Directly over a DME station at 6,000 ft AGL, roughly what will the DME read?
  4. Which RNAV approach type requires WAAS but provides no vertical guidance?
  5. Which hold-entry sector is the narrow 70° one, and on which side of the holding course does it lie?
Show answers
  1. Reads progressively higher than actual airspeed.
  2. Lag (undershoot).
  3. About 1 nautical mile, since DME reads slant range.
  4. LP.
  5. The teardrop sector, on the holding side (the same side as the pattern), between the holding course and the 70° line.
