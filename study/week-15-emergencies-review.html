Day 1

Instrument and System Failures in Flight

Topics: IFH Chapter 11 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe partial-panel flying after a vacuum or attitude-indicator failure
  • State the priority order when handling any in-flight emergency

🧰 Partial panel IR.VII.D.K2

After a vacuum-driven attitude indicator or heading indicator fails (Week 9), you fall back on the instruments that do not share that power source: typically the electric turn coordinator, the magnetic compass, the altimeter, VSI, and airspeed indicator. Bank comes from the turn coordinator, cross-checked against the compass. Pitch comes from the airspeed, altimeter, and VSI trends together, since no single one gives the picture the attitude indicator used to. This is why Week 10's primary/supporting concept matters even more here: you lean harder on whichever instruments are still reliable for each axis IR.VII.D.R1.

Recognize it early IR.VII.D.K1
A failing gyro rarely announces itself. Cross-check the attitude indicator against the turn coordinator, compass, and the other performance instruments. If they disagree, believe the group, cover the suspect instrument, and tell ATC.

🧭 Aviate, navigate, communicate IR.III.A.K2

Maintain aircraft control first, above everything else. Then navigate: know where you are and where you are going. Only then communicate: tell ATC what is happening and what you need. This order does not change just because the emergency is a system failure rather than an engine problem; a perfectly composed radio call from an aircraft that has lost control helps no one. As pilot in command you may deviate from any rule to the extent needed to meet an emergency (91.3), and you must send a written report to the FAA if asked.

🌀 Unusual attitudes IR.IV.B.K1

Recover by...
Nose-high (airspeed decreasing)Add power, lower the nose to level pitch, level the wings.
Nose-low (airspeed increasing)Reduce power, level the wings, raise the nose to level pitch.

Recognize the attitude from the instruments, correct bank and pitch with smooth inputs, and do not rely on a possibly failed attitude indicator for the recovery.

🧭 Worked example

The tumbling attitude indicator

Your attitude indicator tumbles and gives obviously wrong information in IMC.

Resist the urge to keep glancing at it out of habit. Cover it if you have to, fly bank off the turn coordinator and pitch off the airspeed/altimeter/VSI trio, and only once the aircraft is under control tell ATC you have an instrument failure and ask for the help you need, such as vectors to an approach in the best weather.
Self-check
  1. After a vacuum failure, which instrument typically remains your primary bank reference?
  2. What is the standard priority order for handling any in-flight emergency?
Show answers
  1. The turn coordinator (electric).
  2. Aviate, navigate, communicate, in that order.

Day 2

Engine and Electrical Emergencies IFR

Topics: IFH Chapter 11 (continued) · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe the added complications of an engine or electrical failure while IFR in IMC

🔧 Engine failure IFR

The core engine-failure procedure from your private pilot days (best glide, restart checklist, pick a landing area) does not change. What changes is that IMC removes your ability to visually pick a field, so declaring an emergency early and getting ATC's help finding the nearest suitable airport and a vector toward it matters far more than it would VFR. ATC can also provide terrain and obstruction information you cannot see. Squawk 7700 and say so.

⚡ Electrical failure

A full electrical failure can take out radios, some navigation equipment, and any electrically driven gyros, while leaving a vacuum-driven attitude indicator and heading indicator (if so equipped) untouched. That is effectively the reverse of Day 1's partial-panel problem. Without radios, 91.185's lost-comm procedures from Week 2 govern your route and altitude, and you should squawk 7600 if the transponder still works. A handheld transceiver, if carried, can restore two-way communication even with the panel dark.

SquawkMeaning
7500Hijack
7600Lost communications
7700Emergency
Automation and glass IR.II.A.R3
In glass-panel aircraft an electrical failure can remove the primary flight display too. Know what your standby instruments are, how long the battery lasts, and what you can shed to extend it.
🧭 Worked example

An alternator failure in IMC

The ammeter shows a discharge. You are on battery power with a shrinking window before avionics start dropping out.

Prioritize: reduce electrical load (turn off nonessential equipment) and get your position, a plan, and a call to ATC out early, while you still have both radios and enough battery to act on whatever they give you, rather than waiting until the panel actually goes dark.
Self-check
  1. Why does declaring an emergency early matter more for an engine failure in IMC than in VFR conditions?
  2. In an aircraft with a vacuum attitude indicator and an electrical failure, what typically survives?
Show answers
  1. You cannot visually pick a landing field, so ATC's help finding the nearest suitable airport and terrain/obstruction information becomes far more valuable.
  2. The vacuum-driven attitude indicator and heading indicator, since they do not depend on electrical power; radios and electric gyros are what you lose.

Full Content Review: Month 4 Checkpoint

Topics: Full review, practice exam · About 120 min
Learning objectives
  • Review your entire missed-question log from Months 1–3 before the final practice-exam grind

🔍 How to review efficiently

Do not re-read every chapter cover to cover again. Go through your missed-question log topic by topic, re-derive the correct answer from first principles (not just “the answer was C”), and only return to the source material for topics where you are still missing questions in the same category repeatedly. Two months of a running log should have surfaced your two or three genuinely weak areas; spend the bulk of this review there rather than spreading it evenly.

1
Sort the log by topic, and where you can by ACS code (the chips on these pages). The knowledge test report you get after the real test lists the ACS codes of the questions you missed, so tagging your practice misses the same way trains you on the real map.
2
Count misses per topic. The top two or three are your weak areas.
3
For each weak topic, re-derive the answer on paper without looking, then check against the week page.
4
Retest that topic with fresh questions from the bank.
