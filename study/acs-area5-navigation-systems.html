DME Arc Flying

New material — a DME arc is flown by bracketing: small heading corrections (roughly 5°–10°) toward the station if the DME reading creeps outside the arc distance, and away from the station if it creeps inside. The lead radial or lead distance to roll out onto the next course is charted — anticipate it rather than waiting until you're past it.

🌀 Real-World Scenario — Flying a 10 DME Arc

Established on the arc, inbound to a VOR/DME

1
Turn to intercept the arc, then roll out on a heading roughly 90° to the radial you're crossing to establish on the arc.
2
Every 2–3 miles, cross-check the DME: if it reads more than the arc distance (e.g., 10.3 on a 10 DME arc), turn 10°–20° toward the station; if it reads less (9.7), turn 10°–20° away.
3
As you approach the published lead radial for the next course, start your turn early rather than waiting for the CDI to center — by the time it centers on the new course, you're already past it.
Arcs are flown by small, frequent corrections rather than one big course-intercept turn — treat the arc distance the same way you'd hold an altitude: catch the drift early, with small inputs.

🧮 Real-World Scenario — Checking RAIM Before a GPS Approach

Filing a GPS approach to an airport with no ground-based backup approach

Your aircraft has a non-WAAS IFR GPS. Before you depart, you check RAIM availability (through Flight Service or your avionics' own prediction function) for your estimated arrival time at the destination.

If RAIM is predicted unavailable at your ETA, you cannot legally or safely rely on that GPS approach as planned — you need either a different approach at that airport, a different airport, or to adjust your timing. This is a preflight planning step, not something to discover in the terminal area. WAAS-equipped GPS units use a different integrity-monitoring scheme and generally aren't subject to this same RAIM-outage planning step.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.V.A.R1 Automated Navigation

Monitor what the autopilot/GPS is actually doing against what you expect it to do — course reversals and procedure turns are a common place for an autopilot to do something surprising.

IR.V.A.R2 Situational Awareness

A DME arc or a complex RNAV procedure is exactly the kind of task that can absorb your attention at the expense of the bigger picture — altitude, other traffic, fuel.

IR.V.A.R3 Navigation System Limitations

Know the specific limitation of whatever you're using that moment — VOR service volume and station passage cone-of-confusion, or GPS RAIM/database currency.

🛫 ATC Routes, DPs, and STARs IR.V.B.K1

A Departure Procedure (DP) — Obstacle Departure Procedure (ODP) or Standard Instrument Departure (SID) — exists to get you clear of terrain and obstacles with known, charted guidance. Some specify a minimum climb gradient in feet per nautical mile that's steeper than the standard 200 ft/NM — you need to know, before you take the runway, whether your aircraft's performance that day can meet it.

📐 Real-World Scenario — Can You Make the Climb Gradient?

The DP requires 290 ft/NM minimum to 4,000 ft. Can your aircraft comply?

Formula
Required climb rate (ft/min) = climb gradient (ft/NM) × groundspeed (kt) ÷ 60
Given
290 ft/NM required, groundspeed 90 kt on climbout
Calculation
290 × 90 ÷ 60 = 435 ft/min required
Check
Your POH shows a best-rate climb of 700+ fpm at this weight/density altitude — you comply with margin to spare
The catch: climb gradient requirements are built on groundspeed, not airspeed — a strong tailwind on climbout increases the fpm you need to hit the same gradient, and a density altitude that erodes your climb performance works against you at the same time. Both deserve a second look on a hot day or a gusty tailwind departure.

A Standard Terminal Arrival (STAR) similarly carries charted altitude and/or speed constraints at specific fixes — "cross ABCDE at or above 8,000, at or below 10,000, 250 knots or less" is a window, not a suggestion, and you're expected to plan your descent and speed reduction to meet it without a last-second scramble.

📞 Pilot/Controller Responsibilities IR.V.B.K2

ATC provides separation, traffic advisories (workload permitting), and navigation assistance; you retain responsibility for terrain/obstacle clearance on any segment where you're not being radar vectored, for complying with charted procedures, and for telling ATC when you can't meet an instruction — silence is read as compliance.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.V.B.R1 ATC Communications & Procedure Compliance

A missed radio call or a procedure flown slightly wrong is far easier to fix by speaking up immediately than by hoping it goes unnoticed.

IR.V.B.R2 Traffic Avoidance Equipment Limitations

TCAS/TIS-B/ADS-B traffic displays have real gaps (non-equipped aircraft, coverage holes) — they supplement, not replace, a visual scan when conditions allow.

IR.V.B.R3 See and Avoid

Even under IFR and in a radar environment, the responsibility to see and avoid other traffic when visual conditions permit never fully goes away.

📋 Key Takeaways

Fly a DME arc with small, frequent heading corrections (5°–10°) rather than one big turn, and lead the next course before the CDI centers IR.V.A.K1.
Check RAIM availability at your ETA before relying on a non-WAAS GPS approach — this is a preflight step, not an in-flight discovery IR.V.A.K2.
Climb gradient (ft/NM) converts to a required climb rate (fpm) via groundspeed — a tailwind on climbout raises the fpm you actually need IR.V.B.K1.
STAR altitude/speed constraints are a window you plan for in advance, not a last-minute scramble IR.V.B.K1.
