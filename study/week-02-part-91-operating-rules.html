Day 1

IFR Fuel and Alternate Requirements

Regs: 91.167, 91.169 · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • State the three-part IFR fuel requirement and calculate a reserve
  • Apply the 1-2-3 rule to decide whether an alternate is required
  • Apply the standard alternate weather minimums (600-2 / 800-2)
  • State the one exception that lets you skip filing an alternate

⛽ 91.167: IFR fuel requirements IR.I.C.K3c

To depart IFR you need enough fuel, considering weather reports and forecasts, to fly to your first airport of intended landing, then (if an alternate is required) from there to your alternate, then still have 45 minutes of fuel left at normal cruising speed (30 minutes for helicopters). That 45-minute reserve is fixed regardless of how clean the weather turns out to be; it is not something you can skip because the forecast looks good, and it is the same day or night.

“Normal cruising speed” means how you are actually flying that leg, not the airplane's fastest or thirstiest setting. If you are running lean-of-peak at 9.5 gallons an hour, that is your reserve math, not the AFM's high-power fuel-flow figure.

🔢 The 1-2-3 rule and alternates IR.I.C.K1d

The 1-2-3 exception lives in 91.169(b) (and the fuel rule in 91.167(b) mirrors it); 91.169(c) sets the alternate weather minimums. Look at the forecast for your destination from 1 hour before to 1 hour after your ETA. If the ceiling will be below 2,000 feet above the airport, or the visibility below 3 statute miles, at any point in that window, you need an alternate.

Exception: if Part 97 publishes a standard approach for your destination and the 2,000-and-3 forecast holds for the whole window, you can skip the alternate (and the fuel to reach it) entirely.

Once you need an alternate, it has its own weather test at your ETA there:

Best available approach at the alternateMin. forecast ceilingMin. forecast visibility
Precision (ILS, LPV)600 ft2 SM
Non-precision (VOR, LNAV, NDB)800 ft2 SM
No approach publishedMust be able to descend from the MEA to a landing under basic VFR

These are planning minimums for filing, not what you need to land there if you divert. If the plate publishes its own alternate minimums (look for the circled “A”), use those instead of the standard 600-2/800-2.

🧮 Worked example

KAUS to KDFW, ETA 1400Z

The TAF for KDFW shows a 4,000-ft ceiling and 6 SM visibility from 1300Z to 1500Z. Do you need an alternate? Ceiling 4,000 is at or above 2,000 and visibility 6 is at or above 3, so the 1-2-3 test passes; no alternate is required (KDFW has a Part 97 approach).

Now suppose the TAF adds a TEMPO group from 1330Z–1500Z showing an 800-ft ceiling. That group falls inside your ETA window and drops the ceiling below 2,000, so you now need an alternate even though the prevailing forecast alone would have passed. Any qualifying group inside the window counts, not just the base forecast.

You pick KFTW as the alternate; its best published approach is an ILS, so the forecast at your ETA there needs at least 600 ft and 2 SM to file it.

⛽ Fuel math in practice

Autopilot-free, 190 nm, 172 at 110 KTAS

With a 15-knot headwind component, groundspeed is about 95 kt, so 190 nm takes about 2 hours. At 9 GPH that is 18 gallons en route.

Add the 45-minute reserve (about 7 gallons at 9 GPH): roughly 25 gallons minimum with no alternate required. If an alternate is required, add the fuel to fly there first. IR.I.C.R7: the 45 minutes is a floor, not a target.
Self-check
  1. Under 91.167, once you reach your first airport of intended landing and (if required) your alternate, how much more fuel must you have on board?
  2. State the 1-2-3 rule in one sentence.
  3. Your alternate's best approach is non-precision (VOR), with no alternate minimums published on the plate. What is the minimum forecast ceiling and visibility required at your ETA there?
  4. True or false: if Part 97 prescribes a standard approach for your destination and the 2,000-and-3 forecast holds for 1 hour before through 1 hour after your ETA, you do not need to designate an alternate at all.
  5. Is the 45-minute IFR fuel reserve based on the aircraft's maximum-range power setting or on how you are actually operating that leg?
Show answers
  1. 45 minutes at normal cruising speed (30 minutes for helicopters).
  2. An alternate is required unless the destination forecast, from 1 hour before to 1 hour after ETA, shows a ceiling at or above 2,000 ft above the airport AND visibility at or above 3 SM for the entire window.
  3. 800 ft ceiling and 2 SM visibility (the standard non-precision alternate minimums).
  4. True. That is the exception.
  5. How you are actually operating it (actual normal cruise fuel flow), per FAA Chief Counsel guidance, not a theoretical max-range figure.

VOR Currency and IFR Equipment

Regs: 91.171, 91.205 · About 70 min
Learning objectives
  • State the 30-day VOR check requirement and all three check methods with tolerances
  • Recite GRABCARD and explain what each item does
  • Explain how the IFR equipment list builds on the VFR day/night list rather than replacing it

📻 91.171: VOR equipment check IR.V.A.K1

If you are navigating by VOR under IFR, that VOR must have been checked for accuracy within the preceding 30 days. Three accepted methods:

MethodToleranceNotes
VOT or designated ground checkpoint±4°VOT: tune the frequency, set the OBS to 180° FROM or 360° TO. Ground checkpoints are published in the Chart Supplement.
Airborne checkpoint±6°Wider because flying over a fixed point in the air is less precise than sitting on a ground mark.
Dual VOR±4° between the twoTwo independent receivers tuned to the same station, compared to each other. It does not prove accuracy against the true course.

Log the date, place, bearing error, and a signature (yours or an appropriately-rated repair station's) in the aircraft records. If you are navigating entirely by GPS for a given flight, the 30-day VOR check does not apply to that flight, but keep it current for anything relying on VOR.

🧱 91.205: IFR equipment (GRABCARD) IR.II.C.K2

For IFR flight you need everything required for day VFR, plus everything required for night VFR, plus a specific IFR-only list. Most pilots use GRABCARD for that last piece:

G
Generator or alternator
R
two-way Radios, comm and nav suitable for the route to be flown
A
Altimeter, sensitive and adjustable for barometric pressure
B
Ball (slip/skid indicator)
C
Clock, sweep-second hand or digital equivalent
A
Attitude indicator
R
Rate-of-turn indicator
D
Directional gyro (heading indicator)

GPS is not on this list by name; it satisfies the “radios/nav” item as long as it is suitable and approved for the route, alongside or instead of VOR.

🚫 When something is inoperative: 91.213 IR.II.C.K3

If one of these fails before departure and the aircraft has no Minimum Equipment List (most piston singles do not), you run the 91.213 analysis. An item may fly inoperative only if it is not on the aircraft's equipment list required by its type certificate or an AD, not required by 91.205 or the kind of operation, and it is then removed or deactivated, placarded “Inoperative,” and logged by an authorized person, with you deciding it is not a hazard.

The trap
“Inoperative but not required” only applies to equipment that is not on the IFR list. For most of GRABCARD, especially the attitude indicator, the analysis ends with “not legal for IFR today.”
🧮 Worked example

Two equipment drills

Clock: on preflight you find the clock's second hand is broken, but the panel shows GPS time. Legal? Yes: 91.205(d) requires a clock displaying hours, minutes, and seconds, with a sweep-second pointer or digital equivalent, and a working GPS time display satisfies that.

Rate of turn: the turn coordinator is inoperative, but the aircraft has a certified backup attitude system meeting the 91.205(d)(3) exception for a qualifying third attitude instrument. Some glass-panel aircraft are wired this way, so check your specific installation rather than assuming.

Self-check
  1. How many days of currency does a VOR check give you for IFR flight?
  2. What is the tolerance for a VOT or ground-checkpoint check versus an airborne checkpoint check?
  3. Two independent VOR receivers, no VOT or ground checkpoint available. How do you perform a legal check, and what is the tolerance?
  4. Spell out what each letter in GRABCARD stands for.
  5. Is GRABCARD a complete, standalone IFR equipment list?
Show answers
  1. 30 days.
  2. ±4° for VOT/ground checkpoint, ±6° for an airborne checkpoint.
  3. Tune both receivers to the same VOR station and compare the indicated bearings; the maximum permissible variation between them is 4°.
  4. Generator/alternator, Radios, Altimeter (sensitive), Ball, Clock, Attitude indicator, Rate-of-turn indicator, Directional gyro.
  5. No. It is only the IFR-specific additions on top of the day-VFR list and the night-VFR additions; IFR requires all three layers together.

Day 3

Clearances, Course, and Cruising Altitude

Regs: 91.173, 91.179, 91.181 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • State what 91.173 requires before IFR flight in controlled airspace
  • Correctly apply 91.179 in both controlled and uncontrolled airspace, without importing the VFR +500 rule
  • State the 91.181 course-tracking rule

📋 91.173: ATC clearance and flight plan required IR.III.A.K1

You cannot operate under IFR in controlled airspace unless you have filed an IFR flight plan AND received an appropriate ATC clearance. Both parts matter: filing alone is not enough, and you cannot receive an IFR clearance without having filed. This is the line between “flying IFR” and “flying on an instrument flight plan you never activated.”

🛫 91.179: IFR cruising altitude IR.I.C.K2

Two completely different regimes:

AirspaceRule
ControlledFly the altitude or flight level ATC assigned. There is no odd/even calculation; you fly the number in your clearance, unless you are cleared “VFR conditions on-top,” which sends you back to the VFR rule in 91.159.
Uncontrolled, below 18,000 MSL, magnetic course 000–179°Any odd thousand: 3,000, 5,000, 7,000…
Uncontrolled, below 18,000 MSL, magnetic course 180–359°Any even thousand: 2,000, 4,000, 6,000…
The +500 trap
There is no +500-foot offset for IFR. That offset belongs to the VFR hemispheric rule (91.159). Mixing the two up is probably the single most common wrong-answer trap on this topic.

🧭 91.181: course to be flown

On a charted airway you fly the centerline. On any other route you fly the direct course between the navaids or fixes that define it, unless ATC authorizes otherwise. The reg specifically does not stop you from maneuvering to stay clear of traffic, or to intercept a climb or descent path in VFR conditions.

🧮 Worked example

Which altitude?

You are IFR at 6,000 in Class E (controlled) airspace, cleared “direct KABC, maintain 6,000.” What altitude are you legally required to fly? 6,000, exactly what ATC assigned, regardless of your magnetic course.

For practice only: same flight, but in uncontrolled airspace on a magnetic course of 250°, below 18,000 MSL, choosing your own IFR altitude. Legal choices are even thousands: 4,000, 6,000, or 8,000. No +500.

Self-check
  1. What two things does 91.173 require before you can fly IFR in controlled airspace?
  2. You are in controlled airspace on an IFR clearance, level cruise, no special ATC instruction. What altitude must you fly?
  3. Uncontrolled airspace, IFR level cruise, magnetic course 095°, below 18,000 MSL. What altitudes are legal?
  4. On a Federal (Victor) airway, exactly where must you track under 91.181?
  5. True or false: the 500-ft offset (odd/even + 500) applies to IFR aircraft.
Show answers
  1. A filed IFR flight plan and a received, appropriate ATC clearance.
  2. Whatever altitude ATC assigned, not a computed odd/even altitude; the hemispheric odd/even rule for IFR only applies in uncontrolled airspace.
  3. Any odd-thousand MSL altitude, e.g. 5,000, 7,000, 9,000; no +500 offset.
  4. Along the centerline of the airway, unless ATC authorizes otherwise; off an airway, the direct course between the navaids or fixes defining the route.
  5. False. That offset is the VFR hemispheric rule (91.159); IFR uses plain odd/even thousands with no offset.

Takeoff and Landing Under IFR, Minimum Altitudes

Regs: 91.175, 91.177 · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • Recite the three-part test for descending below DA/DH or MDA
  • List several qualifying visual references and the 100-ft approach-lights-only rule
  • State the 1,000-ft / 2,000-ft minimum IFR altitude rule and the MEA/MOCA exception

🛬 91.175(c): operating below DA/DH or MDA

You may not continue below the authorized DA/DH (precision) or MDA (non-precision) unless all three of these hold, continuously:

1
Position: you are continuously positioned to make a normal-rate, normal-maneuver landing on the intended runway. High, fast, and diving for it is a go-around, not a save.
2
Flight visibility: what you can actually see from the cockpit is at or above the visibility published for that approach. Reported visibility (ATIS, tower) is informative, but flight visibility is the legal test.
3
Visual reference: at least one specified reference for the intended runway is distinctly visible and identifiable.

The qualifying references: the approach light system, runway threshold, threshold markings, threshold lights, REIL, VASI, touchdown zone, TDZ markings, TDZ lights, the runway itself, runway markings, and runway lights.

The 100-ft rule
If the approach lights are the only thing you can see, you cannot go below 100 ft above the touchdown zone elevation unless you also pick up the red terminating bars or red side-row bars (or another qualifying reference). It is a favorite trick question.

⛰️ 91.177: minimum altitudes off the published structure IR.I.C.K2

Away from a charted MEA/MOCA, the floor is 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 4 NM of your course in non-mountainous terrain, or 2,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 4 NM in a designated mountainous area. If both an MEA and a MOCA are published for a segment, you can go below the MEA down to, but not below, the MOCA, as long as you are within 22 NM of the VOR that defines the segment.

🧮 Worked example

Two quick calls

MOCA: MEA on a segment is 7,000, MOCA is 4,500, and you are 18 NM from the defining VOR. Legal to fly 5,000? Yes: you are within 22 NM and 5,000 is at or above the 4,500 MOCA.

Approach lights only: on an ILS you have the approach lights in sight at 250 ft above the touchdown zone, but nothing else. Can you continue below 100 ft above TDZE on the lights alone? No, not unless you also pick up the red terminating bars, red side-row bars, or one of the other qualifying references by then.

Self-check
  1. Name the three conditions that must ALL be met before descending below DA/DH or MDA.
  2. Name at least four acceptable visual references for condition 3 above.
  3. If all you can see is the approach light system, how low can you legally continue before you need a qualifying reference beyond the lights themselves?
  4. Off-airway, non-mountainous terrain, no MEA/MOCA published. What is the minimum IFR altitude?
  5. Same question, designated mountainous terrain.
Show answers
  1. (1) Continuously positioned for a normal-rate, normal-maneuver landing on the intended runway. (2) Flight visibility at or above what is published for that approach. (3) At least one specified runway-environment visual reference distinctly visible and identifiable.
  2. Any four of: approach light system, runway threshold, threshold markings, threshold lights, REIL, VASI, touchdown zone, TDZ markings, TDZ lights, runway, runway markings, runway lights.
  3. 100 ft above the touchdown zone elevation.
  4. 1,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 4 NM of the course.
  5. 2,000 ft above the highest obstacle within 4 NM of the course.

Comms, Malfunctions, and Lost Comm

Regs: 91.183, 91.185, 91.187 · About 80 min
Learning objectives
  • State what must be reported under 91.183 and 91.187
  • Recite AVEF (route) and the highest-of-three altitude rule (91.185) from memory
  • Walk a full lost-comm scenario end to end

🎙️ 91.183: routine IFR reporting IR.III.A.K1

In controlled airspace, keep a continuous watch on frequency and report: the time and altitude passing each designated reporting point (or only the ones ATC specifically asks for, if you are under radar control), any unforecast weather you encounter, and anything else relating to safety of flight.

⚠️ 91.187: malfunction reports

If your navigation, approach, or communication equipment malfunctions in flight while IFR in controlled airspace, report it to ATC as soon as practical. The report needs four things: aircraft identification, the equipment affected, the degree to which your IFR capability is impaired, and the nature and extent of assistance you want from ATC. This is the reg that turns a GPS RAIM failure or a dead ILS receiver into a required call, not an optional one.

📻 91.185: two-way radio communications failure IR.III.A.K3

If comms fail in VFR conditions, or you can get to VFR, just continue VFR and land as soon as practicable. That is the easy half. In IMC, you fly a specific route and a specific altitude until your clearance limit, then a specific procedure to get down. Two memory aids carry almost the whole reg.

AVEF
Route, in priority order: Assigned in your last ATC clearance; Vectored, meaning if being radar vectored, the direct route from where the failure happened back to the fix, route, or airway named in that vector clearance; Expected, the route ATC said to expect in a further clearance; Filed, the route on your flight plan.
ALT
Altitude, on each route segment, fly the HIGHEST of: the last assigned altitude, the minimum IFR altitude for that segment, or the altitude ATC said to expect in a further clearance. Segment by segment, not just whichever you remember first. If ATC gave an “expect X at time/fix Y,” you owe them that altitude at that time or fix even with no radio.

At your clearance limit: if it is a fix from which an approach begins, start the approach at your expect-further-clearance (EFC) time, or if none was given, as close as possible to your filed ETA. If it is not an approach fix, leave the clearance limit at your EFC and proceed to a fix that does begin an approach, timed the same way. Do not descend below the minimum safe altitude until established on the approach. Squawk 7600.

PIC emergency authority IR.III.A.K2

91.3(b): in an in-flight emergency requiring immediate action, the PIC may deviate from any rule in Part 91 to the extent required to meet that emergency, including a clearance. ATC may ask for a report afterward.

Similar call signs IR.III.A.R4

“N123AB” and “N123BA” on one frequency is a documented hazard. Use your full call sign on every readback and listen for yours specifically.

🧮 Worked example

The classic lost-comm scenario

Assigned altitude 7,000, cleared direct on a route where a minimum IFR altitude of 9,000 applies partway along. Comms fail. You climb to 9,000 at the point that minimum applies, even with nobody talking to you.

Later you cross onto an airway segment with an MEA of 5,000. What do you fly? Not 5,000. You stay at 7,000, because your last-assigned altitude beats that segment's MEA in the highest-of-three test. You only descend when a segment's own MEA or expected altitude actually exceeds 7,000, or once you are established on the approach.

Self-check
  1. In a non-radar environment, what must you report at each designated reporting point?
  2. Besides reporting points, name the two other things 91.183 requires you to report.
  3. Your ILS receiver fails in flight while IFR in controlled airspace. What are you required to do, and what four things go in the report?
  4. Radio fails while IFR in IMC. Using AVEF, what is the priority order for the route you fly?
  5. Same scenario. What altitude do you fly for a given route segment?
  6. At your clearance limit, if it is a fix from which an approach begins and you never received an EFC time, when do you start the approach?
Show answers
  1. The time and altitude passing that point.
  2. Any unforecast weather encountered, and any other information relating to the safety of flight.
  3. Report to ATC as soon as practical; include aircraft identification, the equipment affected, the degree of IFR-capability impairment, and the nature and extent of assistance desired from ATC.
  4. Assigned route, then (if being vectored) the direct route from the point of failure to the fix/route/airway in the vector clearance, then the route ATC said to expect, then the filed route.
  5. The highest of: the last assigned altitude, the minimum IFR altitude for that segment, or the altitude ATC said to expect in a further clearance.
  6. As close as possible to your estimated time of arrival, calculated from your filed (or last ATC-amended) estimated time en route.

  4. Yes: within the 30-day window and within the ±4° tolerance for a ground checkpoint check.
  5. The attitude indicator; it is a primary flight instrument required by 91.205(d), and that almost never survives the 91.213 analysis for IFR.

  7. The centerline of the airway, unless ATC authorizes otherwise.
  8. Yes; the threshold and REIL are both on the qualifying visual-reference list.
