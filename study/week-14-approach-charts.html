Day 1

Approach Plate Anatomy

Topics: IPH Chapter 4 (first half) · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • Label the major elements of an instrument approach plate from memory

🗺️ The four parts of a plate

Briefing strip

Top of the page: approach name, frequencies, final approach course, MSA, airport and touchdown zone elevation, and notes.

Plan view

The overhead picture: fixes, transitions, holding, the MSA circle, and the course to the runway.

Profile view

The side picture: altitudes at each fix, the glidepath or descent, and the missed approach.

Minimums

The DA or MDA and visibility for each line of minimums and each aircraft category.

🔎 The elements, in the order you use them IR.VI.A.K1

MSA
Minimum Safe / Sector Altitude. Emergency-only obstacle clearance (1,000 ft) within 25 NM of the reference point, in the plan view's corner circle. Not for routine use, and it does not guarantee navigation signal reception.
PT
Procedure turn. A course reversal used to get established inbound when you are not already aligned with the final approach course. Not required if you are being radar vectored to the final approach course, or if the plate says NoPT for your transition.
SF
Stepdown fix. A charted fix on the final segment that lets you descend to a lower altitude once past it. Common on non-precision approaches.
FAF
Final approach fix. Where final descent begins. On a non-precision approach it is a charted fix marked with a Maltese cross; on a precision approach it is glideslope intercept, marked with a lightning bolt.
MAP
Missed approach point. The point where, if you do not have the required visual references, you must execute the missed approach. On an ILS it is the DA; on a non-precision approach it is a charted fix, navaid, or time.
Min
Minimums lines. The DA or MDA and required visibility for each approach category and each level of equipment (straight-in vs. circling, ILS vs. LOC-only, LPV vs. LNAV, and so on).
🧭 Worked example

A stepdown fix on final

On a non-precision approach you cross the FAF at 2,200 ft and descend to the intermediate altitude of 1,800 ft. The MDA is 1,400 ft, and a stepdown fix sits between you and the runway.

You may descend to 1,400 ft only after crossing the stepdown fix, not before, however confident you feel visually. Between the FAF and the stepdown fix, 1,800 ft is your floor.
Below the MDA IR.VI.A.R7
Operating below the MDA without the required visual references is its own named risk in the ACS. The rule is in 91.175(c), reviewed on Day 3.
Self-check
  1. What is the MSA for, and what is it explicitly not for?
  2. When is a procedure turn not required?
  3. What symbol typically marks the FAF on a non-precision approach plate?
  4. What is the Missed Approach Point?
Show answers
  1. Emergency-only obstacle clearance within 25 NM of the reference point; it is not for routine descent planning and does not guarantee signal reception.
  2. When being radar vectored to the final approach course, or when the plate specifies NoPT for your transition.
  3. A Maltese cross.
  4. The point where, without the required visual references, you must execute the missed approach.

🔄 Circling approach categories IR.VI.D.K1

Approach category is based on VREF at maximum certificated landing weight, or 1.3 × VSO if VREF is not published.

CategorySpeed (knots)Approx. circling radius
ALess than 911.3 NM
B91 to 1201.5 NM
C121 to 1401.7 NM
D141 to 1652.3 NM
E166 and above4.5 NM

Radii are the standard TERPS values and vary with airport elevation on actual charts. The speed ranges are what you need for the test.

Never a lower category than you fly
If you fly the approach or the circle faster than your category's upper limit, you must use the minimums for the next higher category, since the protected circling area is sized for that speed range. You can never use a lower category than the speed you actually fly, even if the aircraft is normally certificated in a lower one.
🧭 Worked example

A gusty circle

Your aircraft is certificated Category B, but gusty conditions have you flying the circle at 125 knots.

125 knots is above Category B's 120-knot limit, so you must use Category C circling minimums. The wider turns at that speed need the larger Category C protected area.
Self-check
  1. Is DA a level-off altitude or a decision point in a continuous descent?
  2. What approach types use an MDA rather than a DA?
  3. Category B aircraft circling at 125 knots. Which category minimums must be used?
Show answers
  1. A decision point in a continuous descent; you do not level off at DA.
  2. Approaches without vertical guidance: LOC-only, VOR, LNAV, LP, NDB.
  3. Category C (121-140 knots), since the actual speed flown exceeds Category B's upper limit.

Day 3

Missed Approach Procedures and Practice

Topics: IPH Chapter 4 (missed approach) · About 75 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe the standard missed approach sequence
  • Practice flying labeled approach plates, including the missed approach

👀 What you need to see: 91.175(c)

You may not operate below the DA or MDA unless all of the following are true:

1
The aircraft is continuously in a position from which a descent to a landing on the intended runway can be made at a normal rate, using normal maneuvers.
2
The flight visibility is not less than the visibility prescribed for the approach.
3
At least one required visual reference is distinctly visible and identifiable: the approach light system, threshold, threshold markings or lights, REIL, visual approach slope indicator, touchdown zone or its markings and lights, or the runway, its markings, or its lights.
The approach-lights-only exception
If the approach light system is the only reference you can see, you may descend to no lower than 100 ft above the touchdown zone elevation, and no lower unless the red terminating bars or red side row bars are also visible.

⬆️ The missed approach, in sequence IR.VI.C.K1

1
At the MAP without the required references (or any time you discontinue the approach): apply go-around power and establish a climb.
2
Configure for the climb (flaps and gear per the POH).
3
Follow the charted missed approach procedure exactly as published, unless ATC has already issued alternate instructions.
4
Tell ATC you are missing the approach and what you need (another approach, holding, or an alternate).

The published procedure exists to keep you clear of obstacles during the climb from that particular approach, so fly it as charted rather than improvising, even when you feel you could see enough to do something else. If you go missed before the MAP, continue to the MAP along the final approach course before turning, unless told otherwise IR.VI.C.R4.

Missing the approach while circling IR.VI.D.R7
If you lose sight of the airport while circling, make an initial climbing turn toward the landing runway, continue the turn until established on the missed approach course, and then follow the published missed approach.
🧭 Worked example

Approach lights only at DA

You reach the DA on an ILS with only the approach lights in sight, no red terminating bars, and you cannot see the runway environment.

The lights alone let you continue descending, but only to 100 ft above the touchdown zone. If you still have nothing more by then, go missed, flying the published procedure and not an improvised climbing turn toward where you think the runway is.
Self-check

  2. Why is it important to fly the published missed approach procedure exactly as charted, rather than improvising?
  3. Approach lights are the only reference you see at DA. How low may you descend?
Show answers
  1. Apply go-around power and establish a climb.
  2. It is specifically designed for obstacle clearance during the climb from that particular approach.
  3. To 100 ft above the touchdown zone elevation, and no lower unless the red terminating bars or red side row bars are also visible.
