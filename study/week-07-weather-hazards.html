Day 1

Turbulence and Obstructions to Visibility

Topics: AWH Chapter 18 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Name the main sources of turbulence a GA IFR pilot will encounter
  • Explain how fog forms and why it matters for IFR planning

🌪️ Turbulence sources IR.I.B.K3g

SourceCause
MechanicalWind flowing over rough terrain or buildings.
ThermalConvective currents from surface heating; worst on warm afternoons.
FrontalLifting along a front, especially a fast cold front.
WakeFrom preceding aircraft; strongest behind heavy, slow, clean aircraft.
Clear air turbulence (CAT)Near the jet stream and the tropopause, with no visual warning at all since there is no cloud to see it in.

🌫️ Fog and obstructions to visibility IR.I.B.K3j IR.I.B.K3l

Fog typeHow it formsHow it behaves
RadiationClear, calm night: the ground radiates heat away and cools the air just above it to its dew point.Typically burns off after sunrise as the ground reheats.
AdvectionWarm, moist air moves horizontally over a colder surface (common near coastlines).Does not depend on nighttime cooling; the wind keeps bringing in moist air, so it can persist through the day.
UpslopeMoist air is forced up rising terrain and cools adiabatically.Persists while the upslope flow continues.
Precipitation-inducedWarm rain falls through cooler air near the surface and evaporates into it.Tied to the precipitation area.

Smoke, haze, blowing dust or sand, and volcanic ash also cut visibility without necessarily showing as cloud, and volcanic ash is a serious airframe and engine hazard distinct from normal IMC.

🌫️ Worked example

Fog that will not burn off

A coastal airport reports fog persisting through the middle of the day despite a steady onshore breeze.

Fog that does not burn off with the sun, is wind-driven, and sits near water points to advection fog rather than radiation fog. It will not clear until the wind shifts or the air mass changes, unlike radiation fog, which you can reasonably expect to lift by mid-morning.
Self-check
  1. Which turbulence type has no visual warning at all and occurs near the jet stream?
  2. What causes radiation fog, and when does it typically clear?
  3. How does advection fog differ from radiation fog in terms of persistence?
Show answers
  1. Clear air turbulence (CAT).
  2. Ground cooling on a clear, calm night radiates heat away and cools the air at the surface to its dew point; it typically clears shortly after sunrise as the ground reheats.
  3. Advection fog is driven by wind bringing warm, moist air over a cold surface, so it does not depend on nighttime cooling and can persist through the day.

Day 2

Icing

Topics: AWH Chapter 19 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Name the three icing types and the conditions that produce each
  • Explain why structural icing is dangerous beyond just added weight

🧊 The three icing types IR.I.B.K3i

TypeForms fromLooks and behaves like
Clear iceLarge supercooled droplets (freezing rain, or cumuliform clouds) that spread out before freezingA hard, glossy layer that is hard to see and hard to remove. Generally considered the most hazardous.
Rime iceSmall supercooled droplets (typically stratiform clouds) that freeze almost instantly on contactA rough, opaque, brittle deposit that traps air; easier to see and often easier to remove.
Mixed iceA mix of large and small droplets, or cumuliform clouds with varying droplet sizes at different altitudesHas characteristics of both.

⚠️ Why icing is dangerous

Added weight is the least of it. Ice disrupts airflow over the wing, reducing lift and increasing stall speed, sometimes dramatically, even with what looks like a thin layer. It can also jam control surfaces, block pitot-static ports, and add drag that degrades climb and cruise performance. Structural icing requires visible moisture and a temperature at or below freezing (supercooled droplets can exist well below freezing without freezing until disturbed, which is exactly what an airframe does to them).

Frost IR.I.B.K3k
Frost forms on a cold-soaked airframe in humid air even when the ambient temperature is above freezing. It disrupts airflow over the wing enough to be a takeoff hazard, and it must be removed, not just “brushed off.”
🧊 Worked example

Rime or clear?

You pick up a thin, rough, milky-white deposit on the leading edge while in a stratus layer at −8°C. That description (rough, opaque, stratiform cloud) is classic rime.

If instead you picked up a smooth, clear, hard-to-see coating while in freezing rain below a warm layer aloft, that is clear ice, the more dangerous of the two even though it might look less alarming.

🧊 Real-World Scenario — The Encounter

Level at 6,000, inside a cloud layer, in a non-FIKI 182

Twenty minutes into a cloud deck the forecast did not flag for icing, you notice a thin rime buildup on the wing strut, maybe a quarter inch. Your airplane has pneumatic boots, but it is not certified for flight into known icing; the POH is clear they are for getting out of an inadvertent encounter, not staying in one.

1
Cycle the boots per your POH and confirm the ice is shedding.
2
Ask ATC for a different altitude or a climb/descent out of the icing layer, toward warmer air below if terrain allows, or above the tops if you know them.
3
Turn on pitot heat if it is not already on, and watch the airspeed indicator for the first sign of a pitot blockage.
The equipment bought you time and options; it did not make the airplane icing-capable. IR.II.A.R1 is this exact distinction.
🌩️ Real-World Scenario — The Go/No-Go Call

Saturday morning fly-out

Your destination TAF shows the ceiling dropping to 1,500 broken with 4SM visibility around your arrival time. An AIRMET Zulu covers your route for icing between 6,000 and 10,000 ft, with the freezing level forecast at 7,000 ft. A PIREP from 20 minutes ago reports light rime ice at 8,000 ft along your route.

None of this makes the flight illegal, but a non-FIKI trainer, a destination ceiling near your personal minimums, and a PIREP confirming the exact hazard the AIRMET warned about adds up to a no-go or a different altitude or route, not a “let's see how it looks” decision. IR.I.B.R1 is this judgment call.
Self-check
  1. Which icing type is generally considered most hazardous, and why?
  2. What cloud type is rime ice most associated with?
  3. Beyond added weight, name two ways icing degrades safety.
Show answers
  1. Clear ice; it is smooth, hard, hard to remove, and hard to see, and it forms from larger supercooled droplets that spread before freezing.
  2. Stratiform clouds (small, uniform supercooled droplets).
  3. Any two of: disrupted airflow/increased stall speed, jammed control surfaces, blocked pitot-static ports, added drag/reduced climb performance.

Day 3

Thunderstorms

Topics: AWH Chapter 20 · About 60 min
Learning objectives
  • Describe the three thunderstorm life-cycle stages and the specific hazard of each
  • State the recommended lateral clearance from a severe thunderstorm

⛈️ Thunderstorm life cycle IR.I.B.K3h

StageWhat is happeningHazard
CumulusContinuous updrafts building the cell; no precipitation reaching the ground yet.Rapid vertical growth and turbulence.
MatureUpdrafts and downdrafts side by side; heavy rain, hail, lightning.The most severe turbulence, hail, and microburst potential. The most hazardous stage by far.
DissipatingDowndrafts dominate throughout the cell; rain tapers off.Do not assume it is safe: gust fronts and residual turbulence can persist after the visible storm looks like it is winding down.

📏 Staying clear

The standard guidance (AIM) is to avoid any thunderstorm identified as severe, or giving an intense radar echo, by at least 20 miles, especially under the anvil of a large cumulonimbus, and to give every cell a wide berth. Hazards are not confined to the visible cloud: hail can fall miles from the core, and a gust front (the leading edge of the storm's outflow) can produce severe low-level wind shear well ahead of the visible precipitation. A microburst is an intense, short-lived downdraft with extreme wind shear that has caused fatal accidents on approach.

📡 Worked example

A line of cells on datalink

A line of cells shows a solid red/magenta core on datalink weather with an anvil spreading downwind. Your planned route passes 12 NM from the visible core.

For a cell this intense, 12 NM is inside the recommended 20-mile buffer, and the anvil direction tells you which side is more likely to have hail or turbulence extending beyond the core itself. Remember that datalink radar is also several minutes old: use it for strategy, not for threading between cells.
Self-check
  1. Which thunderstorm stage has both updrafts and downdrafts present simultaneously, and why is that significant?
  2. Is a dissipating-stage thunderstorm necessarily safe to fly near?
  3. What is the standard recommended lateral clearance from a severe thunderstorm or one giving an intense radar echo?
Show answers
  1. The mature stage; the collision of updrafts and downdrafts produces the most severe turbulence, hail, and microburst potential.
  2. No; gust fronts and residual turbulence can persist even as visible precipitation tapers off.
  3. At least 20 miles, and more is better; give every cell a wide berth.
