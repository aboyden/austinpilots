Aircraft Systems Related to IFR Operations

References: 14 CFR part 91; AC 91-74; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -25; POH/AFM
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with anti-icing or deicing systems, and other systems related to IFR flight.

❄️ Anti-Icing and Deicing Systems IR.II.A.K1

🕹️ Flight Control Systems IR.II.A.K2

Most light GA airplanes actuate primary flight controls through mechanical cables, pulleys, and/or pushrods connecting the yoke/stick and rudder pedals directly to the control surfaces. Trim is typically a separate, smaller mechanical or electric system (electric trim run from a switch on the yoke, or a manual trim wheel/cable) that adjusts a trim tab or repositions a spring. An autopilot interfaces with these same control surfaces through electric servos (often friction-clutched capstans on a cable run), which the pilot can physically overpower and must be able to disconnect instantly (yoke-mounted disconnect button, autopilot master switch, or circuit breaker) if it misbehaves — a runaway trim or autopilot hardover is a known IMC emergency scenario.

🧊 Real-World Scenario — The Encounter

Level at 6,000, inside a cloud layer, in a non-FIKI 182

You're level at 6,000 ft, inside a cloud deck the forecast didn't flag for icing. Twenty minutes in, you notice a thin rime buildup on the leading edge of the wing strut — maybe a quarter inch. Your 182 has pneumatic boots, but they're not FIKI-certified; the POH is clear they're for getting out of an inadvertent encounter, not staying in one.

1
Cycle the boots once ice has built a little (too early and it can bridge over them) and confirm it's shedding.
2
Ask ATC for a block altitude or climb/descend out of the icing layer — toward warmer air below if terrain allows, or above the tops if you know them.
3
Turn on pitot heat if it isn't already on, and watch the airspeed indicator for the first sign of a pitot blockage.
The equipment bought you time and options — it didn't make the airplane icing-capable. IR.II.A.R1 is this exact distinction: boots or TKS on a non-FIKI airplane are for executing an exit, not for continuing to fly as planned.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.II.A.R1 Operating in Icing Conditions

Know whether your aircraft is certified for flight into known icing (FIKI) or not — most training aircraft are not, and anti-/de-ice equipment on a non-FIKI aircraft is for exiting an inadvertent encounter, not dispatching into forecast icing.

IR.II.A.R2 Limitations of Anti-/De-Icing Systems

Every system above has a finite capacity (fluid, electrical, or cycle-timing) and does not eliminate ice everywhere on the airframe (antennas, windshield edges, and unprotected surfaces still accrete).

IR.II.A.R3 Automated Systems in IMC

An autopilot reduces workload but can mask a developing problem (e.g., it will quietly trim against an ice-induced control force until it runs out of authority, then disconnect abruptly). Hand-fly periodically and monitor trim position.

Electrical Systems

A typical single-engine IFR aircraft has one engine-driven alternator (or generator) charging a battery through a bus, protected by circuit breakers/fuses. Some aircraft split the bus into an "essential" side (feeds flight instruments, nav/com, and a standby attitude source) and a "non-essential" side (feeds things like cabin lighting) so that a partial electrical failure can be isolated by shedding non-essential loads. Know your aircraft's battery-only endurance for its essential bus — on an all-glass panel, that number is the real answer to "what happens if the alternator fails in IMC."

Primary Flight Display (PFD) and Multi-Function Display (MFD)

A PFD presents the same information as the round-dial six-pack — attitude, airspeed, altitude, heading, VSI, turn rate — as a single electronic display, usually driven by an Air Data, Attitude, and Heading Reference System (ADAHRS) rather than individual vacuum/electric gyros. An MFD typically shows a moving map, engine data, and supplementary navigation information. Many systems have a reversionary mode: if the PFD fails, the MFD can be reconfigured to show the primary flight instruments, and a separate battery-powered standby attitude indicator provides a last-resort backup independent of the main electrical bus.

Transponder and ADS-B

The transponder replies to secondary radar interrogation with your assigned code and, in Mode C/S, pressure altitude — this is how ATC and TCAS-equipped aircraft see your altitude. ADS-B Out broadcasts your GPS-derived position, altitude, and velocity automatically and is required in the airspace specified by 14 CFR 91.225 (generally Class A, B, and C airspace, and above/within the Class B/C "Mode C veil," among other defined areas) — know the rule by airspace, not just "everywhere." ADS-B In lets your own avionics receive FIS-B weather/NOTAMs and TIS-B traffic for free, broadcast on 978 MHz (UAT) or 1090 MHz (1090ES) depending on your equipment.

📡 Real-World Scenario — Do I Need ADS-B Out for This Flight?

Departing a small non-towered field, routing through Class C into a Class B shelf

You're departing a non-towered grass strip (no ADS-B requirement there), climbing through Class C airspace around a regional airport, and your route grazes the outer shelf of a nearby Class B.

The strip itself doesn't require it — but the moment you enter that Class C airspace, or operate within the lateral boundaries of the Class B's surface area (or above it, up to 10,000 ft MSL, per 91.225's "Mode C veil"), ADS-B Out is required. "I'm not landing at the Class B airport" doesn't exempt you — the rule is about the airspace you're in, not your destination.
📏 Real-World Scenario — Why DME ≠ Ground Distance

Flying directly over a VOR/DME at 6,000 ft, with the station at 500 ft elevation

Your height above the station is 5,500 ft, or about 0.9 nautical miles straight up. Because DME measures the direct, line-of-sight (slant-range) distance to the station, not the distance along the ground, your DME readout at the moment you're directly overhead won't show 0.0 nm — it will show approximately 0.9 nm, purely from your altitude above the station.

The error shrinks fast as ground distance grows (at 10 nm out, the same 5,500 ft altitude difference barely moves the reading), which is exactly why the effect matters most close to the station — and why DME-based step-down fixes near the field are worth double-checking against other cues.

🧭 Navigation Systems IR.II.B.K2

K2a VOR

How it works, the CDI, radials, intercepting/tracking, service volumes, and receiver accuracy checks. → Module: VOR Navigation (all sections)

K2a DME

UHF paired-frequency system giving slant-range distance (not ground distance — matters most close to a high-altitude station) plus groundspeed and time-to-station, usually paired with a VOR or ILS.

K2a ILS

Precision approach aid combining a localizer (course guidance, VHF, shares the 108.10–111.95 MHz band with VOR on the odd-tenths frequencies) and a glideslope (vertical guidance, UHF, paired automatically with the localizer frequency). Categories I/II/III reflect progressively lower approach minimums with additional aircraft/crew/airport requirements.

K2a Marker Beacons

75 MHz fan-shaped beacons keyed to specific ILS fixes: Outer Marker (blue light, low tone, dash-dash-dash), Middle Marker (amber light, medium tone, alternating dot-dash), Inner Marker (white light, high tone, dot-dot) — largely supplanted by DME/GPS fixes on modern charts but still found on older installations.

K2b RNAV, GPS, WAAS, FMS, Autopilot

RNAV

Area navigation — direct waypoint-to-waypoint flight rather than station-to-station along airways; the broad category that GPS and FMS both fall under.

GPS

Satellite-based position fixing. A non-WAAS IFR GPS relies on Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring (RAIM) to detect a bad satellite geometry/signal before you trust the position — RAIM can be predicted preflight and can be unavailable at times, which matters for planning a GPS approach.

WAAS

Wide Area Augmentation System — ground reference stations correct GPS signal errors and broadcast the correction via geostationary satellite, improving both accuracy and integrity enough to support vertically-guided LPV approaches with ILS-like minimums, without any ground equipment at the airport.

FMS & Autopilot

An FMS (mostly turbine/advanced aircraft) integrates navigation, performance, and flight planning into one system. An autopilot reduces manual workload (heading/nav/approach/altitude modes) but must still be actively monitored — know your specific modes and disconnect method cold, since IMC is the worst time to be figuring it out.

📱 IR.II.B.K3 Electronic Flight Bag (EFB)

If you fly with an EFB (tablet-based charts/weather/flight planning), know its battery endurance and have a charging plan, keep a second, independent source of current charts (a backup tablet/phone app, or paper) in case the primary fails or its subscription lapses, and mount it securely so it can't become a loose hazard in turbulence. Part 91 operations don't require FAA authorization to use an EFB the way air carrier operations do, but the same good practices apply.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.II.B.R1 Automated Systems

Monitoring and managing autopilot/FMS behavior rather than treating it as "set and forget."

IR.II.B.R2 Approved vs. Non-Approved Devices

A handheld/portable GPS or tablet app is generally not approved as a sole means of IFR navigation — know what's installed and certified in your aircraft versus what's a convenience backup.

IR.II.B.R3 Failure Conditions

Know, for each mode of navigation you rely on, what it looks like when it fails or degrades, and what you'd fly instead.

IR.II.B.R5 Navigation Databases

GPS/FMS navigation databases expire (typically every 28 days) — flying an out-of-date database for an instrument approach is a real, common violation, not just a paperwork technicality.

✈️ Real-World Scenario — Strapping In

A minute-by-minute cockpit check before an IFR departure

1
Master on. The attitude indicator starts to tumble upright — you note the time and plan to recheck it in a few minutes rather than taxiing immediately.
2
Set the altimeter to the current ATIS setting. It reads 495 ft against a field elevation of 542 ft — a 47 ft difference, comfortably inside the 75 ft tolerance.
3
During taxi, the heading indicator reads 270° on a taxiway you know runs east-west — it agrees with the compass, so no precession has crept in overnight.
4
Lined up for takeoff, you note both ASIs (student and instructor side) coming alive together as the aircraft accelerates — if one lagged, this is the moment to abort, not a "we'll watch it" item.
None of this takes more than a minute, and all of it happens before you're relying on any of these instruments in cloud — which is the entire point of IR.II.C.K1.

📜 IFR Airworthiness IR.II.C.K2

Required Inspections

1
91.411 — pitot-static system, altimeter, and altitude reporting equipment tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months for IFR flight in controlled airspace.
2
91.413 — transponder tested and inspected within the preceding 24 calendar months.
3
91.171 — VOR equipment checked within the preceding 30 days if VOR navigation will be used.
4
91.409 — annual inspection (and 100-hour if operated for hire/instruction), plus any applicable Airworthiness Directives.
5
91.207 — ELT and its battery within inspection/replacement limits.

Required Equipment for IFR — 91.205(d)

IFR equipment requirements are cumulative: you need everything in 91.205(b) (day VFR) and (c) (night VFR, if applicable), plus the (d) items below. A common mnemonic is GRABCARD:

G
Generator/alternator of adequate capacity.
R
Radios — two-way communication and navigation equipment suitable for the route to be flown.
A
Attitude indicator (gyroscopic pitch-and-bank).
B
Ball — slip-skid indicator.
C
Clock displaying hours, minutes, and seconds, with a sweep-second pointer or digital equivalent.
A
Altimeter — sensitive, adjustable for barometric pressure.
R
Rate-of-turn indicator (gyroscopic), with a limited exception when a suitable third attitude/heading reference system is installed.
D
Direction indicator (gyroscopic heading indicator or equivalent).

Also carry current aeronautical charts/data for the route (91.503/general airmanship expectation, reinforced in Task I.C), and the aircraft's Certificate of Airworthiness, registration, POH/AFM, and weight-and-balance data as applicable to the aircraft.

🚫 Flying with Inoperative Equipment IR.II.C.K3

14 CFR 91.213 gives you two legal paths when something is inoperative:

1
Aircraft has an approved Minimum Equipment List (MEL): operate strictly within the MEL's conditions, limitations, and time limits for the inoperative item.
2
No MEL (typical for GA): the item may fly inoperative only if it is not (i) part of the VFR-day type-certification instrument/equipment list or an Airworthiness Directive requirement, and (ii) not required by 91.205 or otherwise required for the specific kind of operation (e.g., IFR) being conducted. If it clears both tests, it must be deactivated or removed, placarded "Inoperative," and the work and determination logged by a person authorized to perform maintenance (14 CFR part 43) — and the pilot must independently determine it does not constitute a hazard.
The trap
"Inoperative but not required" only applies to equipment that isn't on the 91.205(d) list above and isn't required for IFR. A broken attitude indicator, for instance, can never be legally placarded and flown IFR — it's squarely on the required list.
🔧 Real-World Scenario — Two Burned-Out Items, Two Different Answers

Preflight turns up two things not working

Walking the preflight, you find the landing/taxi light has burned out, and separately, the VSI needle is stuck at +200 ft/min even with the aircraft parked and level.

LIGHT
Not on the 91.205(d) IFR list, not part of the aircraft's day-VFR type-certification equipment, and not an AD item on this airframe. An A&P can placard it "Inoperative" and log the entry — you're legal to depart IFR (daytime; a landing light has its own separate night-operation logic under 91.205(c) if relevant).
VSI
Not on the 91.205(d) list either — the VSI isn't one of the GRABCARD items. By the same logic as the light, it can be placarded and deactivated, and the flight can still legally go IFR.
The two findings get the same legal answer, but think about the risk difference: losing a landing light costs you nothing in cruise or in IMC, while flying with a known-bad VSI removes one of your two altitude-trend instruments during an approach — legal and wise are not the same question. IR.II.C.R1 is asking you to notice that gap.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.II.C.R1 Operating with Inoperative Equipment

Legal under 91.213 is not the same as wise for the specific flight you're about to fly — a placarded backup instrument on a hard IMC day is a different risk picture than the same item on a CAVU currency flight.

IR.II.C.R2 Outdated Navigation Publications/Databases

Expired charts or a lapsed GPS database are easy to overlook and directly affect approach legality and accuracy — check currency as part of every preflight, not just occasionally.

📋 Key Takeaways

Anti-/de-ice equipment on most training aircraft is for exiting inadvertent icing, not dispatching into known icing — know whether yours is FIKI-certified IR.II.A.K1.
The classic six-pack is only part of "instruments" under the current ACS — glass panels, transponders, and ADS-B are explicitly tested too IR.II.B.K1c.
WAAS doesn't just improve GPS accuracy — it unlocks vertically-guided LPV approach minimums without any ground equipment at the airport IR.II.B.K2b.
IFR airworthiness stacks three separate clocks: pitot-static/altimeter and transponder every 24 months, VOR every 30 days IR.II.C.K2.
GRABCARD is the 91.205(d) IFR equipment list, on top of — not instead of — the day/night VFR lists in (b) and (c) IR.II.C.K2.
A required instrument can never be legally "placarded inoperative" for IFR — the 91.213 removal/placard option only applies to equipment that isn't on the required list IR.II.C.K3.
