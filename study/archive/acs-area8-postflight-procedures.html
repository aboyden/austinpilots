Checking Instruments and Equipment

References: 14 CFR part 91; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -25; POH/AFM
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with checking flight instruments and equipment during postflight.

📝 Documenting discrepancies IR.VIII.A.K1

After the flight, check the instruments and equipment you used and record anything that was wrong. Note the item, what it did, and the conditions, in the aircraft maintenance record or squawk system, so a mechanic can find and fix it. The pilot in command is responsible for determining that the aircraft is airworthy before each flight (91.7), and an unresolved discrepancy has to be dealt with, through repair or a proper deferral, before the next flight.

Postflight checkWhy it matters for IFR
Instrument behavior in flightSticky or precessing gyros, a slow VSI, a compass swing, pitot heat or ASI lag. These are the early signs of a bad instrument.
Navigation and communication equipmentDid every radio, GPS, and CDI work? Record VOR accuracy checks and note any warning flags.
Transponder and ADS-BMode C and ADS-B Out are required in much of controlled airspace; log any ATC report that your Mode C was wrong.
Electrical and vacuumLow voltage, ammeter swings, or suction readings that drifted all point to a developing problem.

📝 Real-World Scenario

The VOR that only drifts on the way home

On the instrument flight home, the left VOR seemed to wander 5° against the other. The weather was good, and you assumed it was nothing.

Write it down: which receiver, which station, how many degrees, and the date. Without a record, the next pilot flies an IFR approach with a receiver that fails the dual-VOR check, and the mechanic has nothing to go on. IR.VIII.A.R1 is about doing that after every flight, not just the bad ones.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VIII.A.R1 Performing and documenting the postflight inspection

Skipping the walk-around or the log lets small defects ride into the next flight. Document it while it is fresh.
