Area VII · Task A

Loss of Communications

References: 14 CFR part 91; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with loss of communications while operating solely by reference to instruments.

📻 Lost communications IR.VII.A.K1 IR.III.A.K3

Try to restore communications first, and then follow 91.185. → Week 13: Lost comm re-drill → Week 2: 91.185

1
Troubleshoot: volume and squelch, headset and plug, the correct frequency, the other radio, then the frequency from the chart or a nearby VOR. Try 121.5 and Flight Service.

Squawk 7600 and keep transmitting your intentions in the blind.
3
In VMC (or if you encounter VMC), continue VFR and land as soon as practicable.

In IMC, follow 91.185 for route and altitude.

⏱️ When to begin the approach

If the clearance limit is a fix from which an approach begins, start the descent and approach as close as possible to the expect-further-clearance (EFC) time, or, if you received no EFC, the ETA calculated from the filed or amended time en route. If the limit is not an approach fix, leave it at the EFC time (or on arrival if no EFC), proceed to a fix from which an approach begins, and start the approach as close as possible to the ETA.

When you may deviate
91.185 sets the default. As pilot in command you may deviate to the extent needed to meet an emergency (91.3), and you should tell ATC as soon as communication returns. IR.III.A.K2
📻 Real-World Scenario

Silence after a handoff

You are in IMC at 7,000 ft on an airway, and the controller's last words were “contact Houston Center on 124.2.” You tune it, call twice, and hear nothing.

Check that you tuned what was said, step back to the previous frequency, try another radio and the 7600 squawk, and think about the cause before you call it a failure. Often the radio is fine and the frequency was wrong. IR.VII.A.R1 is the list of reasons.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VII.A.R1 Possible reasons for lost communication

Wrong frequency, a stuck or unplugged headset, a failed radio, an electrical problem, or being outside coverage.

IR.VII.A.R2 Deviating from the lost-comm procedures

Fly the procedure so ATC can predict you. Improvising removes the one thing controllers can count on.

Area VII · Task B

One Engine Inoperative: Straight-and-Level and Turns

References: FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -25; POH/AFM
Objective: flight solely by reference to instruments with one engine inoperative. Multiengine (AMEL, AMES) only

This task applies only to applicants testing in a multiengine airplane, and it is not part of the instrument knowledge test for a single-engine applicant. The elements are listed here so the ACS is complete.

✈️ Engine failure on instruments IR.VII.B.K1

The sequence is: maintain aircraft control, set the power for the remaining engine, identify and verify the failed engine (dead foot, dead engine, then confirm with the throttle), and feather per the checklist. On instruments there is no horizon to help, so bank toward the operating engine and hold the airspeed at the airplane's single-engine best-rate speed.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VII.B.R1 Identifying the inoperative engine

Verify before you feather. Shutting down the good engine is the classic fatal error.

IR.VII.B.R2 Inability to climb or maintain altitude

Know the single-engine service ceiling and what to do when the airplane cannot hold altitude.

IR.VII.B.R3 Low-altitude maneuvering

Stall, spin, and controlled flight into terrain are the risks.

IR.VII.B.R4 Distractions and disorientation

Task prioritization still comes first: aviate, navigate, communicate.

IR.VII.B.R5 Fuel management

Crossfeed is a decision that follows your checklist, not a reflex.

IR.VII.B.R6 Configuring the aircraft

Clean up drag (gear, flaps) in the order the checklist gives, and do not change configuration while you are still sorting out which engine failed.

Area VII · Task C

Approach and Landing with an Inoperative Engine

References: 14 CFR part 91; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; POH/AFM; Terminal Procedures Publications
Objective: performing an instrument approach and landing with one engine inoperative. Multiengine (AMEL, AMES) only

🛬 Approach with one engine inoperative IR.VII.C.K1

Single-engine approaches are flown with the same stabilized criteria as normal ones, with less performance margin: plan the configuration so you can still go around, and keep the gear and flaps late until you are committed to land. ACS risk items IR.VII.C.R1 and IR.VII.C.R2 are marked archived in the current standard. → Week 14: Missed approach

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VII.C.R3 Configuring the airplane

Delay full flaps and gear until landing is assured, because a single-engine go-around may not be possible in landing configuration.

IR.VII.C.R4 Low-altitude maneuvering

Avoid steep turns and slow speeds near the ground; bank toward the operating engine.

IR.VII.C.R5 Distractions and disorientation

Keep the approach briefing simple, and keep prioritizing control, then navigation, then communication.

IR.VII.C.R6 Go-around or rejected landing with an engine failure

Know before you start the approach whether the airplane can climb on one engine in the landing configuration, and what you will do if it cannot.

Area VII · Task D

Approach with Loss of Primary Flight Instrument Indicators

References: 14 CFR part 91; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; POH/AFM; Terminal Procedures Publications
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing an approach solely by reference to instruments with the loss of primary flight control instruments.

🧰 Recognizing and handling the loss IR.VII.D.K1 IR.VII.D.K2

Recognize inaccurate or inoperative primary instruments by cross-checking them against each other and the standby instruments, then tell ATC or the evaluator. Cover or ignore the failed instrument, and fly with the instruments still working. → Week 15: Partial panel → Week 9: Instruments

FailureRemaining sources
Attitude indicator or vacuumTurn coordinator and compass for bank; airspeed, altimeter, and VSI for pitch. Expect to lose the heading indicator too.
Electrical (electric gyros)Vacuum attitude and heading indicators may remain, but radios and the turn coordinator may be lost.
Glass primary displayStandby attitude indicator, altimeter, and airspeed; reversion to the other display.

A common answer to a primary-instrument loss is a non-precision approach or a radar-assisted no-gyro approach, where the controller calls “turn left/right” and “stop turn,” and you make standard-rate turns until established on final, then half-standard-rate turns.

🖥️ Real-World Scenario

A blank primary flight display

On a glass-panel airplane the PFD goes dark in IMC at 5,000 ft. The standby attitude indicator and the multifunction display are still working.

Fly the standby attitude indicator first, and then tell ATC. Ask for the simplest approach available, with vectors, and configure early so the workload at low altitude stays small. IR.VII.D.R1 and IR.VII.D.R2 are exactly this.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VII.D.R1 Secondary flight displays

Know where they are and how they differ before you need them.

IR.VII.D.R2 Maintaining aircraft control

Control first; the approach comes after.

IR.VII.D.R3 Distraction and disorientation

Prioritize; reduce workload by asking ATC for help and the simplest approach.
