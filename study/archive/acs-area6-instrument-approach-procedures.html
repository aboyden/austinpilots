Area VI · Task A

Non-precision Approach

References: 14 CFR part 91; AC 120-108; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; Terminal Procedures Publications
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing non-precision approach procedures solely by reference to instruments.

🧭 Procedures and limitations IR.VI.A.K1

A non-precision approach gives lateral guidance only (VOR, NDB, localizer-only, LNAV, LP). You fly it to an MDA, not a DA, and a descent below the MDA needs the visual references in 91.175(c). → Week 14: Approach Charts

ElementWhat to know
LP vs. LNAVBoth are lateral-only and published to an MDA. LNAV uses GPS with a fixed linear course width on final (±0.3 NM full scale). LP requires WAAS and uses angular (localizer-like) sensitivity that narrows toward the runway; it is published only where terrain or obstacles prevent an LPV.
Visual descent point (VDP)A charted point (a “V” on the profile) from which a normal descent to the runway is possible if you have the references. A VDP does not by itself permit descent below the MDA; you still need the 91.175(c) references.
MAPA fix, a navaid, or a time from the FAF. Timing: minutes = distance ÷ groundspeed × 60 (5 NM at 90 kt is 3:20).
Continuous descent (AC 120-108)Instead of leveling at the MDA, fly a stabilized, constant-angle descent from the FAF and begin the missed approach at or slightly above the MDA so the climb-out never dips below it.

📟 RNAV displays and annunciations IR.VI.A.K2

A GPS changes its sensitivity as you approach. En route the CDI is ±5 NM full scale; within 30 NM of the destination it goes to ±1 NM (terminal mode); about 2 NM before the FAF it goes to ±0.3 NM (approach mode). You should see the approach annunciation (such as LNAV, LP, L/VNAV, or LPV) before the FAF. If the unit downgrades or shows an integrity warning, you fly the minimums for the line you actually have, or go missed. At the MAP the unit typically suspends waypoint sequencing (SUSP); you must resume it to navigate the missed approach.

📡 Systems used IR.VI.A.K3

Ground-based

VOR, VOR/DME, NDB, localizer (LOC), localizer-type directional aid (LDA), SDF. A back-course localizer gives reverse sensing unless your HSI is set up for it.

Satellite-based

GPS (RAIM) and WAAS. An IFR-approved GPS may substitute for DME or ADF fixes, but it does not replace the primary guidance for a VOR or NDB approach unless the procedure is titled “or GPS”.

🎯 Stabilized approach and energy management IR.VI.A.K4

A common stabilized-approach criterion is: by 1,000 ft AGL in IMC (500 ft in VMC) you are on the final approach course, configured, at target airspeed, on the planned descent path, and within a normal descent rate. It is a widely used operating standard rather than a Part 91 rule. For a 3° path, descent rate in feet per minute is roughly groundspeed × 5 (a more precise multiplier is 5.3).

Practical-test standards (Task A)
Before the final segment: altitude ±100 ft, heading ±10°, airspeed ±10 kt, and no more than three-quarter scale CDI deflection. On final: no more than three-quarter scale, airspeed ±10 kt, and altitude MDA +100/−0 ft to the VDP or MAP.
🌫️ Real-World Scenario

High and fast at the FAF

You are cleared for the RNAV (GPS) RWY 17 approach. You cross the FAF 20 knots fast, the gear is still up, and you are above the charted altitude.

That is not a stabilized approach, and fixing it with a steep dive toward the MDA stacks up descent-rate and configuration risk right where there is the least margin. Tell ATC, go missed or request a turn out, and re-establish the energy state. IR.VI.A.R5 is exactly this.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VI.A.R1 Deviating from the approach

Do not cut corners off the charted track or altitudes. If a clearance and the chart disagree, ask.

IR.VI.A.R2 Selecting a navigation frequency

Tune, then identify by Morse code, and confirm the course. A wrong frequency or a missing ident has led to flying the wrong approach.

IR.VI.A.R3 Automation

Know what mode the autopilot or GPS is in and what it will do next, especially at the FAF and MAP.

IR.VI.A.R4 Configuration

Configure early and keep the plan for the missed approach as well, since climb performance changes with flaps and gear.

IR.VI.A.R5 Unstable approach and high descent rates

Brief a target descent rate, and treat a rate above about 1,000 fpm low in the approach as a reason to go around.

IR.VI.A.R6 Deteriorating weather

Decide your bailout before you start: the alternate, the fuel, and the point where you will go missed.

IR.VI.A.R7 Below the MDA without visual references

The rule is 91.175(c). The MDA is a floor, not a target, and the missed approach starts at the MAP.

Area VI · Task B

Precision Approach

References: 14 CFR part 91; AC 90-105, 90-107; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; Terminal Procedures Publications
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing precision approach procedures solely by reference to instruments.

🛬 Procedures and limitations IR.VI.B.K1

An ILS gives lateral (localizer) and vertical (glideslope) guidance to a DA. Fly the localizer and glideslope inside three-quarter scale from the FAF to the DA; at DA, if you cannot unmistakably see and identify the required references, start the missed approach at once. → Week 11: ILS → Week 14: Minimums

Descent rate

Once on the glideslope, the rate is groundspeed × 5, adjusted for wind. Establish that rate where vertical guidance begins so you stay on the path without chasing it.

Intercept from below

Intercept the glideslope from below at the charted altitude. False glideslopes exist at steeper angles above the real one, so check your altitude against the chart at the FAF.

Inoperative equipment

Glideslope out makes it a localizer approach to an MDA. Approach lights or other visual aids out raise the visibility requirement per the table in the front of the Terminal Procedures Publication.

Category I

A decision height no lower than 200 ft above the touchdown zone. Category II and III need special authorization and aircraft equipment.

📟 Displays, modes, and systems IR.VI.B.K2 IR.VI.B.K3

Know the difference between a mode that is armed and one that has captured. An autopilot approach mode that is armed is not yet tracking the localizer or glideslope, and a failure flag on the HSI or CDI means you must stop using that guidance. Verify the ILS ident and monitor the flags. Interference and signal integrity matter: ATC protects the ILS critical area only when the reported weather is below about 800-ft ceiling and/or 2 SM visibility, so a taxiing aircraft or vehicle can disturb the signal in better weather.

🎯 Stabilized approach IR.VI.B.K4

The same criteria as Task A, with the added discipline of a hard decision point at the DA. A DA is a point in a continuous descent; the airplane may momentarily go below it during the missed approach transition, and the procedure design allows for that.

Practical-test standards (Task B)
On final from the FAF to the DA: no more than three-quarter scale deflection of either lateral or vertical guidance, and airspeed ±10 kt. At the DA, go missed immediately if the references are not unmistakably visible.
🌧️ Real-World Scenario

Chasing the glideslope

You are a dot high on the glideslope at the FAF, and you pitch down hard to get back on it. The needle swings through, and now you are a dot low and fast.

Small, early corrections win: a power reduction and a small pitch change, then settle on the rate that holds the needle. Large corrections create a chase you cannot win: at 90 knots you cover 150 ft every second. IR.VI.B.R5 is the unstable approach this leads to.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VI.B.R1 Deviating from the approach

Follow the charted transitions, altitudes, and the course on final.

IR.VI.B.R2 Selecting a navigation frequency

Tune and identify the localizer; the glideslope pairs automatically.

IR.VI.B.R3 Automation

Know the armed and captured modes and what the system does at glideslope capture.

IR.VI.B.R4 Configuration

Gear and flaps set early enough to be stable, with the go-around configuration already in mind.

IR.VI.B.R5 Unstable approach and high descent rates

If you are not stable by your gate, go around.

IR.VI.B.R6 Deteriorating weather

Have the alternate and fuel decision made before you reach the FAF.

IR.VI.B.R7 Descending below DA without references

The DA is a decision, not a suggestion. If you do not see it, you go.

Area VI · Task C

Missed Approach

References: 14 CFR parts 91, 97; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; Terminal Procedures Publications
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing a missed approach procedure solely by reference to instruments.

⬆️ Missed approach elements IR.VI.C.K1

1
Initiate promptly at the MAP (or DA) without the required references, and say “missed approach” to ATC.
2
Power and pitch: go-around power, establish the pitch for a positive climb.
3
Configure per the airplane manual (flaps, then gear when the climb is positive), and accelerate to the climb speed.
4
Navigate: fly the published missed approach procedure exactly, or the alternate ATC gives you, turning only where it is charted to turn.
5
Request what you want next: another approach, the alternate, a hold, or another clearance limit.

If you go missed before the MAP, continue along the final approach course to the MAP before turning, unless ATC gives other instructions. With an FMS or autopilot, remember the sequencing is suspended at the MAP, so you may need to resume it or select the go-around mode and then verify the track. → Week 14: Missed approach

Practical-test standards (Task C)
Airspeed ±10 kt, heading or course ±10°, altitude ±100 ft during the missed approach, and report it to ATC.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VI.C.R1 Deviation from procedures or instructions

If you cannot meet a climb gradient or restriction, tell ATC.

IR.VI.C.R2 Holding, diverting, or trying again

Consider fuel, weather trend, and your own state. A second approach in the same conditions only makes sense if something has changed.

IR.VI.C.R3 Configuration

Flaps and gear change both drag and pitch; plan the order.

IR.VI.C.R4 Going missed early or below minimums

Traffic, a navigation flag, an unsafe landing situation, or an unstabilized approach are all reasons to go around, at any altitude.

IR.VI.C.R5 Automation

Check that the autopilot is doing what you expect at the MAP.

Area VI · Task D

Circling Approach

References: 14 CFR parts 91, 97; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; Terminal Procedures Publications
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing a circling approach procedure.

🔄 Circling procedures and limits IR.VI.D.K1

Circling is a visual maneuver flown at or above the circling MDA after a straight-in approach is not available or not aligned. Use the minimums for your approach category, based on the speed you actually fly. → Week 14: Circling

CategorySpeed (knots)Approx. circling radius
ALess than 911.3 NM
B91 to 1201.5 NM
C121 to 1401.7 NM
D141 to 1652.3 NM
E166 and above4.5 NM

Fly the circle inside the protected area, keep an identifiable part of the airport in sight, and descend below the MDA only when you are in position to make a normal landing. Some airports prohibit circling in certain directions; the chart says “circling not authorized.” If you lose sight of the airport, execute the missed approach with an initial climbing turn toward the landing runway.

Practical-test standard (Task D)
Bank no more than 30°, no descent below the MDA or preselected circling altitude, and no excessive maneuvering.
🌙 Real-World Scenario

Circling at night, 1,000-ft ceiling

Winds favor Runway 35, but the only approach is a VOR to Runway 17 with a circling MDA of 900 ft AGL, ceiling 1,000 broken at night, visibility 3 SM.

That is legal and a high-risk set of conditions: low altitude, a bank limit, no horizon, and a short pad of margin above the MDA. Many pilots set personal minimums above the circling minimums at night. IR.VI.D.R2 and IR.VI.D.R6 are exactly this.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VI.D.R1 Prescribed circling procedures

Stay inside the protected area for your category.

IR.VI.D.R2 Night or marginal visibility

Consider a straight-in alternative, another runway, or a different airport.

IR.VI.D.R3 Losing visual contact

Do not “try to find it again” at low altitude; go missed.

IR.VI.D.R4 Automation

Autopilot altitude capture near the MDA can level you at the wrong altitude.

IR.VI.D.R5 Altitude, airspeed, and distance

Hold speed within your category and your distance from the runway.

IR.VI.D.R6 Low-altitude maneuvering

Stall, spin, and controlled flight into terrain are the real hazards of a steep turn near the ground.

IR.VI.D.R7 Missed approach after the MAP while circling

Climb and turn toward the landing runway, then follow the published missed approach.

Area VI · Task E

Landing from an Instrument Approach

References: 14 CFR part 91; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; POH/AFM
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing procedures for a landing from an instrument approach.

🛬 Pilot responsibilities and factors IR.VI.E.K1

At the DA or MDA you transition from instruments to a visual picture. Wind, runway length and condition, braking action, and the visibility you actually have all affect whether to continue to land from a straight-in or circling approach. If you are not in position to land with a normal descent using normal maneuvers, go around.

💡 Signs, markings, and lighting IR.VI.E.K2

ItemWhat to know
Approach lighting (ALSF-1/-2, MALSR, ODALS, and others)High-intensity ALSF systems have sequenced flashers and, on ALSF-2, red terminating and side row bars. Medium-intensity systems (MALSR) add a runway alignment indicator. The red bars are what let you go below 100 ft above the touchdown zone on approach lights alone.
Runway end identifier lights (REIL)Two flashing lights at the threshold, one on each side.
Visual glide slope: PAPITwo white and two red is on slope. More white is high (four white is well above); more red is low (four red is well below).
Runway markingsThreshold, aiming point (about 1,000 ft from the threshold), and touchdown zone marks.
Airport signsMandatory signs are white on red (holding positions); location signs are yellow on black; direction and destination signs are black on yellow. ILS critical-area holding markings protect the signal.

📐 Landing profiles IR.VI.E.K3

Plan the visual segment from the DA to the aiming point so you touch down in the touchdown zone, with the configuration and airspeed set for the runway and wind.

🌫️ Real-World Scenario

Sinking below the glidepath at the transition

At DA you break out with the runway ahead. Your eyes jump from the instruments to a long, dark runway with few lights. Without noticing, you let the nose settle.

A black-hole-style approach and the pull of the visual scene both tempt you to fly below the glidepath. Cross-check the PAPI or the vertical guidance on the way down, hold the descent rate, and land only if you are stable. IR.VI.E.R2 and IR.VI.E.R3 cover this.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.VI.E.R1 Landing from an unstable approach

Go around.

IR.VI.E.R2 Flying below the glidepath

Use PAPI or the electronic glideslope to hold the path to the aiming point.

IR.VI.E.R3 Transitioning to visual references

Brief the cues you expect, and be ready to go around if the picture is not right.

IR.VI.E.R4 Configuration for landing

Landing configuration is set before the transition, not during it.
