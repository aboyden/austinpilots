Section 1

How VORs Work

A VOR — VHF Omnidirectional Range — is a ground-based radio navigation station that transmits a signal in every direction simultaneously. Your aircraft receiver compares two signals from the station to figure out exactly what direction you're located from it. That's it. Simple concept, powerful result.

VORs transmit on frequencies between 108.0 and 117.95 MHz, which is the VHF band. This is important because VHF is essentially line-of-sight — the signal doesn't bend around the Earth's curvature the way low-frequency navigation aids do. The higher you fly, the farther away you can receive a VOR.

Key Concept
The VOR station transmits a reference phase signal that's the same in all directions, plus a variable phase signal whose timing shifts depending on the direction. Your receiver compares the phase difference between the two — that difference tells it the magnetic bearing from the station to your aircraft.

Every VOR also transmits a three-letter Morse code identifier so you can positively confirm you're tuned to the right station. Always identify the station before navigating with it. If the identifier is silent, the facility may be under test or maintenance — don't use it for IFR navigation.

🔊 Audio Identifier

Three-letter Morse code. Always identify before use. Example: Austin-Bergstrom VOR idents as AUS (·– ··– ···)

📻 Frequency Range

108.0–117.95 MHz VHF. Frequencies 108.0–111.95 MHz are shared with ILS localizers (even tenths = VOR, odd tenths = ILS).

🧲 Magnetic Bearing

VOR bearings are referenced to magnetic north. When you track a radial, you're tracking a magnetic course — no variation correction needed.

📡 Line-of-Sight

Higher altitude = greater reception range. At low altitude close to a station, you may lose signal. The "cone of confusion" directly overhead is a brief signal dropout.

Section 2

Reading the CDI

The instrument you interact with is the Course Deviation Indicator (CDI). It has two controls you manage: the OBS (Omni Bearing Selector) knob to set your desired course, and you read two outputs: the needle deflection and the TO/FROM flag.

The CDI needle tells you which side of your selected course you're on — it does not point toward the station. Think of the needle as pointing toward where you need to go to get back on course. Full deflection is 10° off course. Each dot represents 2°.

Classic Trap
The CDI needle tells you where the course is, not which way to turn. If you're tracking inbound (TO) and the needle goes right, you need to turn right to intercept. But if you're tracking outbound (FROM) and the needle goes right, you also turn right — because in both cases "fly toward the needle." It works the same way regardless of TO/FROM.

The one thing that does change with TO/FROM is what the situation means. A TO flag means if you fly the selected course, you'll fly toward the station. A FROM flag means you'll fly away from it. You get a FROM flag when you're past the station, or when you're on the opposite side of the selected course.

Section 3

VOR Radials

A radial is a magnetic bearing from the station. The 090 radial points due east from the VOR. The 270 radial points due west. Think of radials like the spokes of a wheel radiating outward from the hub. There are 360 of them.

Radials are always named from the station's perspective. "You are on the 045 radial" means you're northeast of the station, and a bearing from the station to you is 045°. To fly toward the station from the 045 radial, you'd fly a course of 225° (the reciprocal).

Memory Trick
Radials radiate FROM the station. "You are on the 270 radial" = you're due west of the VOR. To fly inbound, fly 090°. Set your OBS to 090 and the flag shows TO — you're flying toward the station on the 270 radial inbound.
Section 4

Intercepting and Tracking a Radial

Knowing what the CDI reads is one thing — flying it smoothly is another. On your IFR practical test, you'll need to intercept a specific radial and track it both inbound and outbound. Here's the technique.

Intercepting

Let's say ATC says "track inbound on the Austin 270 radial." That means you need to fly toward the Austin VOR from the west, on the 270 radial. Here's the process:

1
Tune and identify the VOR. Dial in the frequency, listen for the Morse ident, verify the station ID.
2
Set the OBS to the inbound course. For the 270 radial inbound, set OBS to 090° (reciprocal of 270). The flag should show TO.
3
Determine your position. Rotate the OBS until the needle centers with a TO flag. That tells you what radial you're currently on.
4
Turn to an intercept heading. A 30°–45° intercept angle is standard. If you need to intercept from the south, turn to a heading roughly 45° off 090°.
5
Track inbound when the needle centers. As you approach the radial, the needle moves toward center. When it centers, turn to the inbound course (090°) and track.

Tracking with Wind

A steady crosswind will push you off the radial. If you notice the needle drifting, apply a wind correction angle (WCA) by angling into the wind. Start with 5°–10°, observe whether the needle moves back to center, and refine from there. Once centered, reduce the WCA slightly to maintain — you're bracketing the course.

Common Error
Chasing the needle. If the CDI deflects, don't overcorrect with a 30° turn. Gentle corrections keep the needle near center. A big overcorrection creates an S-turn oscillation that's hard to stabilize. Think: small inputs, observe, adjust.

Station Passage

When you fly directly over a VOR, you enter the "cone of confusion" — a brief area directly above the station where the signals are weak and the TO/FROM flag may flutter or show OFF. This is expected. Within seconds, the flag will flip to FROM as you pass over. At that point, you're now tracking outbound — no course change needed, just keep flying.

Section 5

VOR Service Volumes

Not all VORs are created equal. The FAA assigns each VOR a standard service volume (SSV) — a defined 3D cone within which the signal is guaranteed to be reliable for IFR navigation. The three classes are Terminal (T), Low Altitude (L), and High Altitude (H).

Class Altitude AGL Range Typical Use
T Terminal 1,000 – 12,000 ft 25 NM Approaches, terminal procedures
L Low Altitude 1,000 – 18,000 ft 40 NM En route below FL180
H High Altitude 1,000 – 14,500 ft 40 NM En route at and above FL180
14,500 – FL600 100 NM

On en route charts, VOR symbols are labeled with their class. If you're using a Low altitude VOR to navigate at FL240, you're outside its guaranteed service volume — you might still receive it, but you can't rely on it for legal IFR navigation.

IFR Practical
Airways on Low Altitude charts are built around L-class VORs with 40 NM range. On High Altitude charts (jet routes), they're built around H-class VORs with 100 NM range at altitude. The MEA on each airway guarantees VOR reception within those service volumes.
Section 6

VOR Receiver Checks

Before flying IFR, your VOR equipment must be checked within the last 30 days. The regs (FAR 91.171) spell out the acceptable methods and tolerances. You must log the check: date, place, bearing error, and your signature.

Method Tolerance Notes
FAA VOR Test Signal (VOT) ±4° VOT available at select airports. Tune freq, set OBS for 180° with FROM or 360° with TO.
FAA ground checkpoint ±4° Published in Chart Supplement. Taxi to designated spot, tune VOR, check bearing.
Airborne checkpoint ±6° Published in Chart Supplement. Fly over designated fix at published altitude.
Dual VOR check ±4° between the two Both receivers tuned to same VOR. Compare readings. Doesn't confirm accuracy vs. actual course.
Memory Aid — "A VORT of checks"
VOT → Ground → Airborne → Dual. Tolerances: ±4° for VOT, ground, and dual; ±6° for airborne. The airborne check is looser because you can't precisely mark your position over a point as accurately as a fixed ground checkpoint.

📋 Key Takeaways

VORs transmit on 108.0–117.95 MHz VHF. Always identify the three-letter Morse code ident before using for navigation.
Radials radiate FROM the station. To fly inbound on a radial, set the OBS to the reciprocal; the TO flag confirms you're heading toward the station.
The CDI needle points toward the course, not the station. "Fly toward the needle" works for both TO and FROM tracking.
Service volumes: Terminal = 25 NM / 12,000 ft, Low = 40 NM / 18,000 ft, High = 100 NM at altitude.
VOR checks required every 30 days for IFR. Log date, place, bearing error, signature. VOT and ground checks ±4°; airborne ±6°.
Station passage: expect the TO/FROM flag to flutter and briefly show OFF directly over the VOR — this is normal.
