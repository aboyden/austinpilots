Area IV · Task A

Instrument Flight

References: FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with performing basic flight maneuvers solely by reference to instruments.

🎯 Attitude Instrument Flying IR.IV.A.K1

Attitude instrument flying means controlling the aircraft's pitch, bank, and power by interpreting instrument indications rather than a visual horizon. The attitude indicator is the only instrument that shows pitch and bank directly and immediately; everything else (altimeter, VSI, ASI, HI, turn coordinator) either confirms what the AI is telling you or fills in when it's gone.

🧭 Pitch, Bank, and Power Instruments IR.IV.A.K2

Pitch Instruments

Attitude indicator (direct, immediate), altimeter (trend, no lag), VSI (trend, 6–9 second lag), airspeed indicator (trend, in level flight).

Bank Instruments

Attitude indicator (direct, immediate), heading indicator (trend), turn coordinator (direct rate of turn, immediate).

Power Instruments

Manifold pressure and/or tachometer (direct, immediate), airspeed indicator (trend, confirms the power change had the expected effect).

The Scan

A good scan moves continuously between the AI as the hub and the supporting instruments — fixating on any one instrument (including the AI) for too long is how small deviations become large ones unnoticed.

🔍 Real-World Scenario — Catching a Deviation Early

Level at 6,000, in cloud, hand-flying

Your scan touches the AI: wings level, nose on the horizon line — looks fine. But your last three passes across the altimeter showed 6,000, 6,040, 6,080 — climbing slowly, 40 ft at a time. Your VSI confirms +200 fpm.

The AI alone didn't catch this — a pitch attitude just 1–2° nose-high can be nearly invisible on the small AI face but shows up clearly as a trend across the altimeter and VSI over successive scan passes. IR.IV.A.K3 ("normal and abnormal instrument indications") is exactly this: knowing what a *developing* deviation looks like before it's a big one, not just recognizing an instrument that's failed outright.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.IV.A.R1 Physiology and Scan Degradation

Fatigue, hypoxia, dehydration, and even a stuffy nose (affecting the inner ear) all degrade your scan rate and judgment before you necessarily feel "impaired."

IR.IV.A.R2 Spatial Disorientation

The inner ear lies convincingly in IMC — a sustained turn can feel like straight flight, and leveling the wings afterward can feel like a turn the other way. Trust the instruments, not the seat of your pants.

IR.IV.A.R3 Unfamiliar Displays

A scan built on round dials doesn't automatically transfer to a PFD tape display, and vice versa — get dedicated practice on whatever you'll actually fly in IMC.

Area IV · Task B

Recovery from Unusual Flight Attitudes

References: FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -25; POH/AFM
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with recovering from unusual flight attitudes solely by reference to instruments.

🛠️ Recovery Procedures IR.IV.B.K1

AttitudeRecognize byRecovery order
Nose-high Airspeed decreasing, altimeter/VSI showing a climb, AI showing nose above the horizon Power (add, to prevent/recover from an impending stall) → level the wings (coordinated aileron/rudder) → lower the nose smoothly to the level-flight attitude → re-trim
Nose-low Airspeed increasing rapidly, altimeter/VSI showing a descent, AI showing nose below the horizon Power (reduce, to prevent overspeed/excess structural load) → level the wings (coordinated aileron/rudder) → raise the nose smoothly to level flight → re-trim
Why "smoothly" matters
An abrupt pull from a nose-low attitude can turn a bad situation into a secondary stall or an overstressed airframe. The goal is a positive, coordinated correction — not the fastest possible one.

🛡️ Prevention IR.IV.B.K2

Unusual attitudes come from flight-causal factors (turbulence, wake encounters, distraction during a busy phase), physiological factors (spatial disorientation, fatigue), and environmental factors (icing changing handling characteristics, convective turbulence) — plus straightforward system or equipment failures (an AI that tumbles, an autopilot that disconnects unexpectedly). Prevention is mostly about scan discipline and not letting a distraction (a dropped chart, a frequency change) interrupt it for too long.

☁️ Regaining VMC After Inadvertent IMC IR.IV.B.K3

If a VFR flight (or a VFR pilot) ends up in inadvertent/unintended IMC, the standard guidance is: don't try to turn back toward where you came from by visual reference — commit to the instruments, wings level, and either execute a standard-rate 180° turn by instrument reference to exit the way you came, or climb/descend to a known safe altitude and get ATC help, rather than attempting any maneuver by outside visual cues that no longer exist.

🤖 Use of Automation IR.IV.B.K4

An autopilot can be the fastest, most reliable way out of an unusual attitude or inadvertent IMC if it's functioning and you know how to engage it correctly (level mode / straight-and-level or a basic heading+altitude mode) — but only if you've practiced using it for exactly this before you need it. Engaging an unfamiliar mode in a bad attitude can make things worse.

🌀 Real-World Scenario — Working It Through

AI shows 20° nose-up, airspeed decaying through 70 knots, VSI pegged up

1
Recognize: this is nose-high — airspeed decaying and VSI/altimeter both show a climb.
2
Power: add power toward full, to arrest the airspeed decay before it reaches stall.
3
Wings: check the AI's bank indication and the turn coordinator; level the wings with coordinated aileron and rudder.
4
Pitch: smoothly lower the nose toward the horizon line on the AI as airspeed recovers, then re-trim for level flight.
The order matters: adding power first buys you margin against the stall while you're still sorting out bank and pitch — pulling the nose down first, before power is in, can aggravate the speed problem rather than fix it.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.IV.B.R1 Loss of Control In-Flight (LOC-I)

Stress, task saturation, an inadequate scan, and distraction are the precursors — LOC-I is consistently one of general aviation's leading fatal accident categories.

IR.IV.B.R3 Operating Envelope

Know your aircraft's maneuvering speed and structural limits — a recovery that's correct in sequence can still overstress the airframe if executed too aggressively.

IR.IV.B.R6 Control Input Errors

An incorrect or reversed control input during a disorienting moment can turn a minor deviation into the unusual attitude itself.

IR.IV.B.R9 Distraction and Situational Awareness

The same task-saturation that causes an unusual attitude can also prevent you from recognizing you're in one — build in deliberate pauses to re-scan.

📋 Key Takeaways

The AI is the hub of the scan, but a slow trend across the altimeter/VSI over several scan passes can catch a deviation the AI alone won't show IR.IV.A.K3.
Nose-high: power, then wings, then lower the nose. Nose-low: reduce power, then wings, then raise the nose — same wing-leveling step in the middle, opposite power and pitch moves IR.IV.B.K1.
Inadvertent IMC: commit to the instruments immediately — don't try to turn back by visual reference that no longer exists IR.IV.B.K3.
An autopilot can be your fastest way out of a bad attitude, but only if you've practiced the exact mode you'd use before you need it IR.IV.B.K4.
