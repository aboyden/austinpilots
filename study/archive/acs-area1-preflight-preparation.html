Area I · Task A

Pilot Qualifications

References: 14 CFR part 61; AC 68-1; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -25
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with requirements to act as pilot-in-command under instrument flight rules.

🪪 Certification, Recency, and Recordkeeping IR.I.A.K1

Instrument Currency — 6 HITS in 6

To act as PIC under IFR or in weather below VFR minimums, within the preceding 6 calendar months you need 6 instrument approaches, holding procedures and tasks, and intercepting/tracking courses via navigational electronic systems (14 CFR 61.57(c)). These can be done in the aircraft, a full flight simulator, or an approved flight training device (FTD/AATD) with a safety pilot or instructor as required.

Lapsed Currency — the 6/6/12 Window

If you lapse currency, you have an additional 6 months (months 7–12) to regain it with a safety pilot, without an instructor. Beyond 12 months total, you need an Instrument Proficiency Check (IPC) with a CFII, examiner, or other authorized person (61.57(d)) — there's no more "self-currency" option.

Recordkeeping

Log the date, type and location of each instrument approach, and the name of the safety pilot if one was required (61.51(g)). A safety pilot is required whenever you're under a view-limiting device and the aircraft is not equipped with functional dual controls, or more precisely whenever simulated instrument conditions are flown — the safety pilot must hold at least a private pilot certificate with the category/class rating for the aircraft, per 61.51 and 91.109.

Certification Basics

An instrument rating requires a minimum 50 hours of cross-country PIC time (at least 10 in an airplane, if seeking the airplane rating), 40 hours of actual/simulated instrument time, and the required training events under 61.65, plus the knowledge and practical tests.

🧭 Real-World Scenario — The Lapsed Pilot

Maria's currency clock

Maria flew her 6th approach of the cycle on March 1. Life gets busy and she doesn't fly another approach for a while. Here's what the calendar actually allows her to do:

THRU SEP 1
Month 6: She's fully current. Any IFR flight is legal, no safety pilot or instructor needed.
SEP–MAR
Months 7–12: She's lapsed, but still inside the 6/6/12 window. She can fly 6 approaches, holding, and course tracking with a safety pilot — no instructor required — and be legal again the moment she finishes.
AFTER MAR 1
Past month 12: The safety-pilot-only option is gone. She now needs a full Instrument Proficiency Check with a CFII, examiner, or other authorized person — no matter how close she was to finishing on her own.
The lesson: the 6-month and 12-month marks aren't the same deadline with rising urgency — they're two different sets of rules. One extra day past month 12 changes who has to sign off her return to currency.

✅ Privileges and Limitations IR.I.A.K2

The instrument rating removes the restriction in 61.3(e) that would otherwise prohibit you from acting as PIC under IFR or in weather conditions less than the minimums for VFR. Without it, you're limited to VFR (or, for a sport/recreational pilot, additional restrictions apply). The rating doesn't expire on its own — what lapses is your currency under 61.57, which is a separate, renewable requirement from the certificate itself.

⚕️ Part 68 BasicMed Privileges and Limitations IR.I.A.K3

BasicMed (14 CFR part 68) lets a pilot act as PIC without holding a current FAA medical certificate, provided all of the following are met:

1
Hold a U.S. driver's license and comply with any of its medical restrictions.
2
Have held a regular FAA medical certificate at some point after July 14, 2006 (first-, second-, or third-class), and not have had the most recent one revoked, suspended, or denied.
3
Complete a Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist (CMEC) with a state-licensed physician no more than 48 months (4 years) before the flight, and retain the completed checklist.
4
Complete a free online BasicMed medical education course (AOPA or Mayo Clinic) no more than 24 months (2 years) before the flight, and retain the certificate of completion.
5
Operating limits: aircraft authorized to carry no more than 7 occupants total and no more than 6 passengers, with a maximum certificated takeoff weight of 12,500 lbs, operated within the United States, at 250 KIAS or less, at 18,000 ft MSL or below, not for compensation or hire (with the usual limited exceptions), single-pilot operations, and not in operations requiring a type rating.
Know this is a moving target
The weight/passenger limits above were raised by the FAA Reauthorization Act of 2024 (effective November 2024) — BasicMed used to cap out at 6,000 lbs / 5 passengers / 6 occupants. If you're studying from an older book, double-check the current numbers against 14 CFR part 68 before your checkride.
🧑‍✈️ Real-World Scenario — Does Dave Qualify?

Dave wants to fly his Cessna 182 to the coast with his wife and two friends

Dave's 182 is certificated at 3,100 lbs — comfortably under 12,500. He's planning to carry 4 passengers plus himself (5 occupants total), cruising at 8,500 ft and 140 KIAS, entirely within the U.S. He last held a third-class medical back in 2015. Three years ago, his family doctor completed a Comprehensive Medical Examination Checklist with him. He finished the free online BasicMed course 18 months ago.

✓
Weight, occupants, speed, and altitude are all inside the BasicMed limits.
✓
His 2015 medical satisfies "held a medical certificate after July 14, 2006" — it doesn't matter that it expired as a medical certificate years ago.
✓
The CMEC from 3 years ago is inside the 48-month window.
✓
The course from 18 months ago is inside the 24-month window.
Dave is good to go under BasicMed. Change just one fact — say his CMEC was from 5 years ago instead of 3 — and he's grounded under BasicMed until he redoes it, even though everything else checks out.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.I.A.R1 Proficiency vs. Currency

Legally current (6 HITS in 6 months) does not mean proficient. A pilot who scraped by on 6 approaches flown in good VMC with a safety pilot is legal, but may not be ready for an actual approach to minimums in IMC. Be honest about the gap between the two.

IR.I.A.R2 Personal Minimums

Set ceiling/visibility, crosswind, and currency-based minimums for yourself that are more conservative than the regulatory minimums, and raise them further when rusty, tired, or flying an unfamiliar aircraft/route.

IR.I.A.R3 Fitness for Flight

Use IMSAFE (Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Emotion) before every flight. 14 CFR 61.53 prohibits acting as PIC with a known medical deficiency or while using medication that affects faculties in a way contrary to safety — this applies under BasicMed too, even without a medical certificate to invalidate.

IR.I.A.R4 Unfamiliar Aircraft/Avionics

A new-to-you G1000 or glass panel autopilot adds workload exactly when you can least afford it — in IMC. Get dedicated transition training before filing IFR in an unfamiliar avionics suite.

Area I · Task B

Weather Information

References: 14 CFR part 91; AC 91-92; AIM; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -25, -28
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with obtaining, understanding, and applying weather information for a flight under IFR.

🌐 Sources of Weather Data IR.I.B.K1

The two FAA-recognized sources for a preflight weather briefing are the National Weather Service (aviationweather.gov, the authoritative source for raw and graphical products) and Flight Service — operated under contract by Leidos as 1-800-WX-BRIEF / 1800wxbrief.com, which also files and activates/closes IFR flight plans and provides live briefers and in-flight weather via radio. Third-party EFB apps (ForeFlight, Garmin Pilot, ForeFlight's own briefing partner, etc.) typically source the same underlying NWS/Flight Service data through an approved data provider.

📊 Acceptable Weather Products IR.I.B.K2

The ACS requires knowledge of at least three of the following sub-elements:

ProductWhat it tells you
IR.I.B.K2a METAR / SPECI / PIREPMETAR = scheduled hourly surface observation; SPECI = unscheduled special observation when conditions change significantly. PIREP = pilot report of actual conditions (turbulence, icing, cloud tops, etc.) — often the most current, ground-truth data available.
IR.I.B.K2b Surface Analysis Chart / CVASurface Analysis shows current frontal positions, pressure centers, and station plots. The Ceiling and Visibility Analysis (CVA) overlays current ceiling/visibility categories (VFR/MVFR/IFR/LIFR) derived from satellite and surface data.
IR.I.B.K2c TAFTerminal Aerodrome Forecast — a coded forecast for a 5-statute-mile radius around an airport, typically valid 24–30 hours, used to plan destination/alternate weather.
IR.I.B.K2d GFAGraphical Forecast for Aviation — the web-based, graphical replacement for the old textual Area Forecast (FA); shows forecast sky condition, visibility/obstructions, wind, icing, turbulence, and more on an interactive map, in 3-hour increments out to 15 hours (with extended panels further out).
IR.I.B.K2e Winds and Temperatures Aloft (FB)Forecast wind direction/speed and temperature at standard altitudes — critical for TAS/groundspeed calculations, icing risk (freezing level), and oxygen/performance planning.
IR.I.B.K2f Convective Outlook (AC)Storm Prediction Center's Day 1/2/3 outlook for organized severe convective activity — useful for strategic, long-lead-time planning around thunderstorm complexes.
IR.I.B.K2g In-flight Advisories — AIRMET / SIGMET / Convective SIGMETAIRMET (Sierra/Tango/Zulu: IFR conditions/mountain obscuration, turbulence/strong surface wind, icing/freezing level) covers conditions of concern mainly to light aircraft. SIGMET covers more severe hazards (severe turbulence/icing, dust/sandstorms, volcanic ash) affecting all aircraft. Convective SIGMET covers lines of thunderstorms, embedded TS, areas of TS, or severe/hail/wind criteria.

🌡️ Meteorology for IFR Flight IR.I.B.K3

At least three of the following must be analyzed for the planned departure, en route, alternate, and destination phases:

K3a Atmospheric Composition & Stability

Stable air resists vertical motion (smooth, stratiform clouds, poor mixing/haze trapping); unstable air favors convection (cumulus buildups, better mixing, turbulence).

K3b Wind

Windshear (abrupt change in speed/direction with altitude — a major factor near fronts, low-level jets, and convection) and mountain wave (strong downdrafts/turbulence on the lee side of ridges when wind crosses perpendicular to terrain at speed).

K3c Temperature & Heat Exchange

Drives convection, determines the freezing level, and governs density altitude performance effects.

K3d Moisture / Precipitation

Dewpoint spread predicts cloud/fog formation; precipitation type and intensity affect visibility and icing risk.

K3e Weather System Formation

Air masses and fronts (cold/warm/stationary/occluded) — the basic building blocks for forecasting where and when weather will change along a route.

K3f Clouds

Cloud families (low/middle/high, cumuliform/stratiform) hint at stability, icing type, and turbulence potential.

K3g Turbulence

Mechanical (terrain/friction), thermal (convective), wind shear, and wake turbulence — know the causes and how PIREPs/AIRMETs report intensity.

K3h Thunderstorms & Microbursts

Three stages (cumulus/mature/dissipating); microbursts produce extreme, short-duration wind shear that has caused fatal accidents on approach — give all convection wide berth.

K3i Icing & Freezing Level

Structural icing requires visible moisture at or below freezing; know the freezing level along your route and the difference between rime, clear, and mixed ice.

K3j Fog / Mist

Radiation, advection, upslope, and precipitation-induced fog each form under different conditions — know which type you're likely to encounter and how it typically clears.

K3k Frost

Forms on a cold-soaked airframe in humid air even when the ambient temperature is above freezing; disrupts airflow over the wing enough to be a takeoff hazard — must be removed, not just "brushed off."

K3l Obstructions to Visibility

Smoke, haze, volcanic ash, blowing dust/sand — reduce visibility without necessarily showing as cloud, and some (volcanic ash) are a serious airframe/engine hazard distinct from normal IMC.

📺 Flight Deck Weather Displays IR.I.B.K4

Know the capabilities and limitations of your installed datalink weather (ADS-B FIS-B or SiriusXM): FIS-B NEXRAD on ADS-B is typically several minutes old by the time it displays (it is not real-time), has no cell-phone-style continuous update, and should never be used for close-in thunderstorm penetration decisions — it's a strategic, not tactical, tool.

🌩️ Real-World Scenario — The Go/No-Go Call

Saturday morning fly-out

Your destination TAF shows the ceiling dropping to 1,500 broken with 4SM visibility right around your arrival time. An AIRMET Zulu covers your route for icing between 6,000 and 10,000 ft, with the freezing level forecast at 7,000 ft. A PIREP from 20 minutes ago reports light rime ice at 8,000 ft along your route.

None of this makes the flight illegal — but a non-FIKI trainer, a destination ceiling already near your personal minimums, and a PIREP confirming the exact hazard the AIRMET warned about adds up to a "no-go" or "different altitude/route" decision, not a "let's see how it looks" one. IR.I.B.R1 is exactly this judgment call — the regulations don't make it for you.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.I.B.R1 Go/No-Go & Divert Decisions

Covers R1a circumstances favoring diversion, R1b your personal weather minimums, and R1c hazardous weather including known/forecast icing or turbulence aloft. Decide your "out" before you need it.

IR.I.B.R2 Use & Limitations

Of R2a installed onboard weather equipment, R2b aviation weather reports and forecasts (all are probabilistic, not guarantees), and R2c in-flight weather resources (datalink lag, radio reception gaps).

Area I · Task C

Cross-Country Flight Planning

References: 14 CFR part 91; AIM; Chart Supplements; FAA-H-8083-2, -3, -15, -16, -25; IFR Enroute Charts; NOTAMs; IFR Navigation Charts
Objective: the applicant exhibits satisfactory knowledge, risk management, and skills associated with planning an IFR cross-country and filing an IFR flight plan.

🧭 Route Planning IR.I.C.K1

a
Available navigational facilities along the route — VOR, GPS/RNAV waypoints, and the airway or direct/RNAV routing they support.
b
Special use airspace — MOAs, restricted/prohibited areas, warning areas — and whether they're active for your transit time.
c
Preferred routes published in the Chart Supplement for busy city pairs, which ATC will often assign or expect you to file.
d
Primary and alternate airports — runway lengths, approaches available, and fuel/services.
e
Enroute charts (IFR Low/High) for airway structure, MEAs, MOCAs, and reporting points.
f
Chart Supplements for airport/facility data, communications frequencies, and special notices.
g
NOTAMs for runway/taxiway closures, approach outages, TFRs, and obstruction changes not yet charted.
h
Terminal Procedures Publications (TPP) — the approach plate/SID/STAR booklet for your departure, en route, and arrival airports.

⛰️ Altitude Selection IR.I.C.K2

Account for terrain and obstacles (use the MEA, MOCA, or your own obstacle clearance — 1,000 ft in non-mountainous terrain, 2,000 ft in designated mountainous areas, per 91.177 for off-airway/direct routing), single-engine glide distance over inhospitable terrain, the IFR cruising altitude rule (14 CFR 91.179 — magnetic course 0°–179° flies odd thousands, 180°–359° flies even thousands, below FL180), the effect of wind on fuel and groundspeed, and oxygen requirements (14 CFR 91.211: supplemental oxygen required for the required flight crew above 12,500 ft MSL cabin pressure altitude for that portion of flight over 30 minutes, required at all times above 14,000 ft, and required for every occupant above 15,000 ft).

🧮 Calculations IR.I.C.K3

K3a Time, Climb/Descent, Course, Distance, Heading, TAS, Groundspeed

Standard E6B/flight-computer work: correct indicated airspeed for altitude/temperature to get TAS, then apply the wind triangle to get heading and groundspeed.

K3b ETA, Including UTC Conversion

IFR flight plans and clearances are typically worked in Coordinated Universal Time (Zulu) — know your local-to-UTC offset and how to convert quickly.

K3c Fuel Requirements, Including Reserve

14 CFR 91.167: enough fuel to reach the first airport of intended landing, then to the alternate (if one is required), then 45 minutes at normal cruising speed — the same 45-minute figure applies day or night under IFR.

📝 Elements of an IFR Flight Plan IR.I.C.K4

Whether filed on paper (FAA Form 7233-1) or electronically, the core fields are the same: aircraft identification; aircraft type and special equipment suffix; true airspeed; departure point and proposed departure time; requested cruising altitude; route of flight; destination and estimated time en route; an alternate airport (if required); fuel on board; remarks; pilot's name, address, and phone number; number of persons on board; and aircraft color. Equipment suffixes communicate your navigation/surveillance capability (e.g., RNAV/GPS, DME, transponder with Mode C, ADS-B Out) to ATC's automation.

📡 Activating and Closing an IFR Flight Plan IR.I.C.K5

Controlled Airspace

File with Flight Service or your EFB provider; your plan is activated automatically when ATC issues your clearance (typically via clearance delivery, ground, or tower) and you depart. ATC usually closes it for you automatically upon landing at a towered airport.

Uncontrolled Airspace / Non-Towered Field

You may need to get your clearance by phone or via a remote communications outlet before taxiing, often with a clearance void time if there's no way to get it airborne. After landing at an airport without a tower or reliable ATC/FSS radio contact, you must close the flight plan yourself — by radio before losing contact, or by phone after landing — or search and rescue procedures may be initiated when you don't show up as "closed."

🗺️ Real-World Scenario — Planning KAUS to KGGG

Walking one trip through every piece of Task C

You're flying from Austin-Bergstrom (KAUS) to East Texas Regional (KGGG) in Longview — about 190 nm on a magnetic course of roughly 030°, in a 172 cruising at 110 KTAS.

a–h
Route: no preferred route is published for this city pair, so you expect a direct or short airway-to-direct clearance. You confirm no MOA sits along the way, note KGGG's two runways and its ILS, and check NOTAMs — today's shows KGGG's ILS glideslope out of service, so you brief the localizer-only minimums instead.
ALT
Altitude: magnetic course 030° falls in the 0°–179° range, so you need an odd thousand — 7,000 ft clears the flat Central/East Texas terrain with plenty of margin and sits comfortably above the freezing level from your weather briefing.
CALC
Calculations: 110 KTAS with a 15-knot headwind component nets about 95 kt groundspeed. 190 nm ÷ 95 kt ≈ 2 hours even. Departing at 1400Z puts your ETA at 1600Z. At 9 GPH that's 18 gallons en route, plus 45 minutes' reserve (about 7 gallons) — call it 25 gallons minimum, and you depart with 40 in the tanks.
PLAN
Flight plan: you file N12345, a C172/G, true airspeed 110, departing KAUS at 1400Z, cruising 7,000, direct to KGGG, estimating 2+00 en route, 4+00 fuel on board, 2 souls on board — no alternate required if the destination forecast still meets the 1-2-3 rule when you check it closer to departure.
ACT
Activating/closing: both ends are towered, so your clearance activates the plan on departure and ATC closes it automatically when you land — no phone call needed either way on this particular trip.

⚠️ Risk Management

IR.I.C.R1 Pilot

Your own currency, proficiency, and physical/mental readiness for this specific flight.

IR.I.C.R2 Aircraft

Performance, equipment, and airworthiness limitations for the planned route and conditions.

IR.I.C.R3 Environment

Weather, airports, airspace, terrain, and obstacles — plus external pressures (get-there-itis, schedules, passengers).

IR.I.C.R5 / R6 Service & App Limitations

ATC services (radar coverage gaps, workload-driven delays) and electronic flight planning apps (routing/terrain/fuel estimates are only as good as their data and your inputs) both have limits — cross-check, don't blindly trust.

IR.I.C.R7 Fuel Planning

Build in margin beyond the legal 45-minute reserve for routing changes, holding, or a missed approach — the reserve is a floor, not a target.

Memory Aid — PAVE
Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures — a simple frame for the R1–R4 risk categories that show up across nearly every ACS task.

📋 Key Takeaways

Currency (6 approaches/holding/tracking in 6 months) and proficiency are different things — the ACS tests whether you know the difference IR.I.A.R1.
BasicMed's current limits are 12,500 lbs, 6 passengers, 7 occupants, ≤250 KIAS, ≤18,000 ft MSL IR.I.A.K3 — a 2024 change from the older, lower limits.
You need working knowledge of at least three weather products IR.I.B.K2 and at least three meteorological hazard categories IR.I.B.K3 for any given flight.
IFR fuel reserve is 45 minutes at normal cruise, day or night, after reaching your destination and alternate (if required) IR.I.C.K3c.
Closing your own flight plan at a non-towered field with no ATC/FSS contact is a pilot responsibility, not something that happens automatically IR.I.C.K5.
